Bohemians

Who they were

From 872 to 1526, with a focus on the Hussite era (1419–1471), the codex's Bohemians are the duchy of Bohemia, a kingdom from 1198. It gained Moravia in the 950s or 1019/1029, Lusatia in 1319–29 and 1367, and Silesia in 1327–35. In 872 the Annals of Fulda name Goriwei among several Bohemian dukes: probably Bořivoj I, first recorded duke of the Přemyslid dynasty. Bohemia's name recalls the Celtic Boii; Czechs call the land Čechy. Most Bohemians spoke Czech; from the thirteenth century many towns had German-speaking burghers, and the king was one of the Empire's seven electors.

Rise and height

Bořivoj ruled in the orbit of Great Moravia; his grandson Wenceslas, killed by his brother Boleslav I in 935 (or 929), became the land's patron saint. Two dukes became kings for life (1085, 1158); hereditary kingship followed in 1198, confirmed by Frederick II's Golden Bull of Sicily (1212). Ottokar II (1253–1278) won Austria, Styria, Carinthia and Carniola, but lost them to the German king Rudolf of Habsburg and fell fighting him and his Hungarian allies on the Marchfeld (1278). Wenceslas II's son was murdered in 1306, ending the Přemyslid royal line. After four disputed years, John of Luxembourg took the throne (1310); he died, blind, charging with his knights at Crécy (1346). His son Charles IV (1346–1378), crowned emperor in 1355, made Prague an imperial residence. Under Wenceslas IV, the preacher Jan Hus called for Church reform; condemned as a heretic by a Church council at Constance, he was burned in 1415. On 30 July 1419 Hussites threw councillors from the windows of Prague's New Town hall; revolution followed (Kaminsky 1967).

War and society

Silver from Jihlava and later Kutná Hora, one of Europe's richest mines, paid for royal power; from 1300 it was minted into the Prague groschen, a coin used across Central Europe. In 1348 Charles IV founded the university, the first in Central Europe (Hus was its rector in 1409–10), and Prague's New Town, planned around broad markets.

From 1414 Hus's followers gave lay people the chalice, by then reserved to priests: communion in both kinds, bread and wine (Latin sub utraque specie, hence "Utraquists"). The Four Articles of Prague (1420) also demanded free preaching, clerical poverty and the punishment of public sins; in the 1420s much Church land passed to nobles and towns (Šmahel 1993). Hussite armies, largely townsmen and peasants under lesser nobles such as Jan Žižka, fought from wagon forts (German Wagenburg): chained, boarded wagons crewed by crossbowmen, handgunners and men with flails and polearms, who pulled horsemen down. They used handguns (píšťala, "pipe") more widely and systematically than most of Europe. Short guns called houfnice (from houf, "crowd") fired stones or scattershot at close range into massed troops. Later sources describe wagon columns that could quickly close into a fort (Durdík 1953), and Žižka's Military Ordinance (1423) imposed strict discipline (Heymann 1955). Under Žižka and then the priest Prokop the Great, the Hussites defeated five crusades (1420–1431) and raided their neighbours, reaching the Baltic in 1433 as Poland's allies.

End and legacy

At Lipany (1434) moderate Hussite and Catholic lords crushed the radical Taborite and Orphan field armies; Prokop fell. The Compacts of Basel (1436) allowed the chalice in Bohemia and Moravia, and Sigismund, Wenceslas IV's half-brother and long the Hussites' enemy, was accepted as king. George of Poděbrady, a Utraquist noble, reigned 1458–1471 despite papal hostility. Jagiellonian kings from Poland followed until Louis II died in the Ottoman victory at Mohács (1526) and the Habsburgs succeeded. Czech mercenaries took the wagon fort and handgun abroad: Jan Jiskra's companies held Upper Hungary (roughly today's Slovakia) from 1440 (Engel 2001). The Utraquist church lasted until the 1620s, and "howitzer" derives from houfnice.

design notes and sources

Contested

  • What Hussitism was. Nineteenth-century national historians saw a Czech struggle against German dominance, and Marxist historians a social revolution of peasants and the urban poor; others (Kaminsky 1967; Šmahel 1993) put religious reform at the centre of a revolution that also had social and national sides.
  • Moravia. Some historians date Bohemian rule over Moravia to the 950s, under Boleslav I; others see it as secure only from 1019 or 1029.
  • "Pistol". Major English dictionaries derive the word, through German and French, from the Hussite píšťala; an alternative view links it to the Italian town of Pistoia.

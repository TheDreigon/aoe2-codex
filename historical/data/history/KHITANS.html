Khitans

Who they were

The Khitans (Chinese Qidan) spoke a language related to Mongolian and herded horses, cattle and sheep in the Xar Moron valley, in today's eastern Inner Mongolia. Before 907 their tribes were at times subject to Tang China or to Turkic and Uyghur steppe empires. The codex covers 907–1125, from Abaoji's proclamation as khagan (supreme ruler) to the empire's fall. Their empire, called Liao or "Great Khitan" at different times, joined the eastern Mongolian steppe to the kingdom of Bohai in Manchuria and the Sixteen Prefectures of north China, from Beijing to Datong. Han Chinese far outnumbered the Khitans in it.

Rise and height

Abaoji (907–926) took the Chinese title of emperor in 916 and conquered Bohai in 926. His widow, Empress Yingtian, passed over the heir for a younger son, Deguang, known as Taizong (927–947). For helping the rebel general Shi Jingtang set up the Later Jin dynasty in north China (936), Taizong gained the Sixteen Prefectures. After Shi's successor broke with him, Taizong took Kaifeng, the Later Jin capital, in 947, but risings against his plundering troops forced him to withdraw (Twitchett and Tietze 1994).

China's Song dynasty tried to retake the Sixteen Prefectures in 979 and 986 but was beaten back. Shengzong (982–1031) became emperor as a boy; his mother, Empress Dowager Chengtian, ruled until 1009. The Liao fought Goryeo in Korea (993–1019) and in 1004 rode deep into Song territory. The campaign stalled at Chanyuan, where a Song crossbow killed their leading general. In January 1005 the two courts made peace as equals: the Song paid 100,000 taels (Chinese ounces) of silver and 200,000 bolts of silk a year, and border markets opened. The peace lasted over a century.

War and society

Herds were the Khitans' wealth, and the court moved between seasonal camps (nabo). By regulation, each regular soldier was to bring three horses, a forager and a camp guard, iron armour, horse armour of leather or iron as his means allowed, four bows and 400 arrows, spears and a mace. No rations were issued: riders went out daily to forage by plunder (Wittfogel and Feng 1949). Abaoji's guard grew into the Pishi army, the emperor's elite cavalry, and each emperor founded an ordo, a palace camp of households, herds and guardsmen that outlived him. The tribes supplied most other horsemen, Han and Bohai militias most foot soldiers.

Abaoji had Han officials, but the two-branch government took shape under Taizong and his successor Shizong (947–951): northern officials ran the tribes and palace camps by Khitan custom, southern ones the Han prefectures in the Chinese way. Han and Bohai captives farmed in walled towns on the steppe. The court favoured Buddhism alongside shamanist rites: it printed the Buddhist canon, the collected scriptures, and sent a copy to Goryeo in 1063. Surviving Liao buildings include the 67-metre wooden pagoda at Yingxian, built in 1056 (Steinhardt 1997). Two Khitan scripts, created in 920 and about 925, remain largely undeciphered (Kane 2009).

End and legacy

In 1114 the Jurchens, a Manchurian people subject to the Liao, rebelled under Aguda, who in 1115 proclaimed the Jin ("Gold") dynasty, unrelated to the Later Jin. They took the Eastern Capital in 1116 and, allied with the Song from 1120, the other capitals; in 1125 they captured the last emperor, Tianzuo. Yelü Dashi, of the imperial clan, had broken with Tianzuo in 1124 and ridden west; the Qara Khitai, or Western Liao, that he founded in Central Asia fell to the Mongols in 1218 (Biran 2005). The Khitans' name lives on in "Cathay", medieval Europe's name for northern China, and in Kitai, Russian for China.

design notes and sources

Contested

  • A "conquest dynasty"? Wittfogel and Feng (1949) made the Liao their model of a "conquest dynasty", whose steppe rulers borrowed Chinese institutions without being absorbed; others stress sinicisation, absorption into Chinese culture. Standen (2007) argues that in the tenth century borders moved with the allegiance of local leaders, and that ethnic identity did not decide whom people served.
  • The Liao shi. The official history was compiled in about a year (1343–44) under the Mongol Yuan dynasty, from Liao and Jin drafts now lost, and is often inconsistent. Its large figures for herds and guard armies are treated with caution, and scholars debate how far its tidy north–south division of offices matches practice.
  • 907 or 916? The Liao shi counts Abaoji's reign from 907; some modern works date the dynasty from 916, when he took the Chinese title of emperor.

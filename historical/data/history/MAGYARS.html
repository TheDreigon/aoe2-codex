Magyars

Who they were

Magyar is the Hungarians' own name; "Hungarian" probably comes from Onogur, a Turkic people's name. Their language is Uralic, like Finnish, and closest to Khanty and Mansi in western Siberia. By the ninth century they were mounted herders on the steppes north of the Black Sea (Róna-Tas 1999). The codex covers 895–1526, from the Conquest of the Carpathian Basin (today Hungary and parts of its neighbours) to the battle of Mohács, with its core ending at King Matthias's death (1490). A tribal confederation under a grand prince became, from 1000, the Kingdom of Hungary, whose people came to include Slavs, Vlachs (Romanians), Germans, Cumans and Jews.

Rise and height

Joined by Kabars, rebels from the Khazar empire, the Magyars fought for the East Frankish king Arnulf against Moravia in 892 and for Byzantium against Bulgaria in 894–95. When Bulgaria turned the Pechenegs, another steppe people, against their homes, the Magyars crossed the Carpathians under Árpád (Constantine VII c. 950). The basin they entered held Slavs, Avar remnants and German-speaking settlers, split between Frankish, Moravian and Bulgarian spheres. Their raiders, sometimes hired by Western rulers, reached Saxony, France, Italy and Spain until Otto I defeated them at the Lechfeld (955) and their retreating army was destroyed (Bowlus 2006). Raids on Byzantium ended at Arcadiopolis (970).

Stephen I, son of Grand Prince Géza, was crowned in Hungary on 25 December 1000 or 1 January 1001, with Emperor Otto III's backing and a crown sent by Pope Sylvester II. The Mongols routed Béla IV at Muhi (1241) and ravaged Hungary for a year. When the Árpáds' male line died out (1301), Charles Robert of Anjou (Charles I), grandson of an Árpád princess, won the throne. The Ottomans beat King Sigismund at Nicopolis (1396), but John Hunyadi, regent from 1446, held Belgrade against them in 1456. His son Matthias Corvinus (1458–90) conquered Moravia, Silesia and Vienna.

War and society

Conquest-era warriors were mounted archers, buried with sabres and reflex composite bows of wood, horn and sinew, stiffened with bone plates. Leo VI's Tactica, a Byzantine manual of about 900, describes their volleys and feigned flights but adapts an account of earlier nomads.

Stephen, canonised in 1083, founded bishoprics and the first counties. Each was run from a royal castle, usually of earth and timber, by an ispán (count), who raised its castle warriors and levies (Engel 2001). After 1241 Béla IV promoted stone castles; about a hundred stood by 1270 (Zsoldos 2013). Fifteenth-century fortresses from Belgrade to Jajce held the southern border. Gold florins were struck from 1325–26; in 1327 Charles I let landowners keep a third of the royal mining dues from their estates. With Kremnica as its chief gold town, Hungary became Europe's largest gold producer (Engel 2001).

Pecheneg and Hungarian-speaking Székely border guards and later Cuman auxiliaries kept the steppe bow alive. From 1397 landowners had to equip one archer for every twenty peasant plots. Matthias revived this levy, reportedly to raise hussars: light horsemen first recorded before 1450 and probably Serbian in origin. His standing army, built from the 1460s with many Czech mercenaries, combined cavalry, infantry and artillery. Like Hunyadi, he used wagon forts as mobile defences; his letter of 1481 places handgunners behind men with great shields (pavises).

End and legacy

Unpaid after Matthias's death, his mercenaries were dispersed in 1492. The Ottomans took Belgrade in 1521 and destroyed the royal army at Mohács on 29 August 1526; King Louis II drowned in flight. After they seized Buda in 1541 the kingdom split in three: a Habsburg west and north, an Ottoman centre and an eastern realm, later the Principality of Transylvania. The counties outlived the split, and "hussar" passed into many languages.

design notes and sources

Contested

  • Origins. Linguists agree that Hungarian is Uralic, but its speakers' homeland, when they took up steppe herding and how far they mixed with Turkic-speaking peoples are debated. Róna-Tas (1999) stresses long contact with Turkic peoples, who left hundreds of loanwords, and ancient-DNA studies since 2018 find mixed ancestry among the conquerors.
  • Castles after 1241. How far the Mongol invasion caused the wave of stone castles is debated: Fügedi (1986) tied it to the lesson of 1241–42 and Béla IV's policy, while others see the castles as built against Hungary's European rivals. Zsoldos (2013) accepts the Mongol lesson as the start but finds that fear of castle-building neighbours soon became the main motive, and that the resulting race strengthened the oligarchs: lords who shut the king out of whole provinces around 1300.
  • Matthias's army. The name "Black Army" is recorded only after Matthias's death (1490), and the much-repeated claim that one infantryman in four carried a handgun has no clear early source.

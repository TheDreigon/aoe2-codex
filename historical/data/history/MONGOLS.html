Mongols

Who they were

The Mongols herded horses and sheep on the Mongolian plateau. In 1206 an assembly (quriltai) at the Onon river's source proclaimed Temüjin as Chinggis Khan (Genghis Khan), and his empire called itself Yeke Mongol Ulus, "Great Mongol State". The codex covers 1206–1368: until about 1260 the united empire, from north China to Iran and the Black Sea steppes, then two khanates of Chinggis's grandsons, Kublai's Yuan dynasty in China and Hülegü's Ilkhanate in Iran. The western steppe khanate, later called the Golden Horde, is the codex's Tatars.

Rise and height

Temüjin crushed rival steppe peoples: the Tatars of eastern Mongolia (1202), Kereyids (1203) and Naimans (1204). As khan he attacked the Tangut kingdom of Xi Xia (1209) and the Jin empire of north China, whose court fled south before his generals took Zhongdu (Beijing) in 1215. When a governor of the Khwarazm Shah, ruler of Central Asia and Iran, massacred merchants sent by Chinggis (1218) and the shah killed his envoy, Chinggis invaded (1219–1223). At Nishapur and Merv (1221), says the Persian historian Juvaini, the people were massacred and a few hundred craftsmen deported. Chinggis died in 1227, campaigning against Xi Xia.

His son Ögödei (1229–1241) destroyed the Jin (1234). From 1236 his nephew Batu and the general Subutai led an army west: it subdued the Rus' principalities and in 1241 routed the Poles at Legnica and the Hungarians at Muhi, but withdrew in 1242. The great khan Möngke (1251–1259) sent his brother Hülegü west; he destroyed the castle state of the Shia Nizari Ismailis in Iran (1256) and sacked Baghdad (1258), killing the Abbasid caliph. After Möngke died, the empire broke into four khanates during the succession war between his brothers Kublai and Ariq Böke (1260–1264). At Ain Jalut (1260) Egypt's Mamluks, former slave soldiers, beat a Mongol force left in Syria, but Ilkhanid armies invaded Syria repeatedly until 1312. Kublai named his dynasty Yuan (1271) and ended the Song dynasty of south China by 1279, but his expeditions failed in Japan (1274, 1281), Đại Việt, today's northern Vietnam (1285, 1287–1288), and Java (1293).

War and society

All adult men owed service in decimal units, from tens to ten thousands. The thirteenth-century Secret History of the Mongols says that in 1206 Chinggis regrouped his people into 95 units of nominally a thousand men under his companions, and raised his guard (kheshig) to 10,000. Many guardsmen became commanders and governors. Each rider led several spare horses (remounts), and armies wore enemies down with arrows, feigned retreats and encirclement (May 2007). The papal envoy Carpini (1247) says each warrior needed two or three bows and three quivers; richer men and some horses wore lamellar armour, laced plates of leather or iron. Juvaini says Chinggis treated the great hunt as training for war: for weeks, the army drove game into a closing ring. Chinese and Muslim engineers ran the siege train; two Muslims sent by the Ilkhan Abaqa built the counterweight trebuchets that ended Kublai's siege of Xiangyang (1268–1273).

Ögödei organised the yam, relay stations with fresh horses for envoys bearing a paiza (tablet of authority); censuses registered subjects for tax and levy (Allsen 1987). The khans lent capital to Muslim and Uighur merchant partners (ortoq) and protected the caravan roads (Allsen 1989). They worshipped Heaven (Tenggeri) but exempted Buddhist, Daoist, Christian and Muslim clergy from taxes, and in 1254 Möngke had Christians, Muslims and Buddhists debate at Karakorum (Rubruck c. 1255).

End and legacy

The Ilkhan Ghazan made Islam the state religion (1295); after Abu Sa'id died heirless in 1335, the Ilkhanate broke apart. In China, factional strife, floods and famine fed rebellions from 1351; in 1368 the Ming took Dadu (Beijing) and the last Yuan emperor fled to the steppe, where his heirs ruled on. Descent from Chinggis remained the mark of a legitimate khan for centuries.

design notes and sources

Contested

  • The Mangudai. The Secret History places the Manggud in Chinggis's vanguard in 1203, but the popular story that the Mangudai were a "suicide squad" has no known medieval source.
  • The withdrawal of 1242. Carpini (1247) says the Mongols left Hungary because of Ögödei's death in Mongolia in December 1241. Sinor (1972) objected that Batu never went back to Mongolia and that the Hungarian plain lacked pasture for the army's many horses; Büntgen and Di Cosmo (2016) point to cold, wet conditions in early 1242, whose thaw turned the plain into marsh, cutting pasture and mobility.
  • A mandate to rule the world. Letters of the great khans to Pope Innocent IV (Güyük, 1246) and Louis IX of France (Möngke, 1254, carried by Rubruck) demand submission in the name of Eternal Heaven; historians disagree on whether Chinggis already claimed such a mandate or his heirs formulated it as their conquests grew.

Byzantines

Who they were

The codex's Byzantines are the Eastern Roman Empire from Justinian I's accession (527) to the fall of Constantinople (1453), with its core in about 670–1100. Its people called themselves Rhomaioi, "Romans", and their state Basileia tōn Rhōmaiōn, "empire of the Romans" (Kaldellis 2019); Arabs and Turks called them Rūm. "Byzantine", from Byzantion, the capital's old name, is a label Western scholars spread from the sixteenth century. After the seventh century its heartlands were Anatolia, the southern Balkans and the eastern Mediterranean, peopled mostly by Greek-speaking Orthodox Christians, alongside Armenians, Slavs and others.

Rise and height

Justinian I (527–565) codified Roman law (529–534) and rebuilt Hagia Sophia (532–537). His generals took Vandal North Africa (533–534), a strip of southern Spain and Ostrogothic Italy (535–554), where Belisarius began the war and Narses ended it; plague struck from 541. Heraclius (610–641) beat Persia (627–628) after Constantinople withstood an Avar and Persian siege (626), but the Arabs took Syria, Palestine and Egypt (634–642). The field armies withdrew into Anatolia and were quartered in districts that later took their names, the themes (Haldon 1999). Arab attacks on Constantinople failed in the 670s (possibly the late 660s) and in 717–718, when a hard winter, famine among the besiegers and Bulgar attacks helped Leo III prevail. In the tenth century the future emperor Nikephoros II Phokas retook Crete (961); Cilicia and Cyprus fell in 965, and Antioch in 969. Basil II (976–1025) annexed Bulgaria by 1018.

War and society

The Strategikon (c. 600), a manual attributed to Emperor Maurice, calls for cavalrymen who can shoot and use the lance, and infantry of shield-bearing spearmen backed by archers and javelinmen. Against three centuries of Arab raids, frontier commanders shadowed raiders, raised the alarm with scouts and beacons, and moved people into fortified towns (kastra). Constantinople's land walls (408–413) withstood every foreign assault until 1453. By the tenth century many soldiers of the themes held lands registered for military service, which laws protected from sale (Haldon 1999), and imperial stud farms bred cavalry horses. Nikephoros II's Praecepta Militaria (c. 965) sets armoured horsemen (kataphraktoi) with maces and lances in a blunt wedge, and heavy spearmen (menavlatoi) against enemy cavalry (McGeer 1995). From 988/989 Rus' warriors from Kiev, later joined by Scandinavians and Englishmen, grew into the emperor's Varangian Guard.

From about 672 the standing fleets of oared galleys (dromons) used Greek fire (Pryor and Jeffreys 2006), a burning, petroleum-based liquid projected from tubes (siphons). Theophanes' chronicle credits it to Kallinikos, a refugee architect from Syria; in 717–718 it burned Arab ships. The state ran on gold: its coin, the nomisma, kept its purity from Constantine I to the mid-eleventh century, and the treasury paid soldiers and allies in coin. Charitable foundations, often monastic, ran hospitals (xenones) with salaried doctors, as at the Pantokrator monastery (1136).

End and legacy

Defeat by the Seljuks at Manzikert (1071) and the ensuing civil wars let Turkish groups settle much of Anatolia. Alexios I Komnenos (1081–1118) restored the state, helped by the First Crusade. In 1204 the Fourth Crusade, diverted by debts to Venice and a would-be emperor's promises, broke in from the sea and sacked Constantinople. Nicaea, a Greek successor state, retook it in 1261. The Ottomans, who first crossed into Europe as allies in Byzantine civil wars, took Gallipoli (1354). On 29 May 1453 Mehmed II stormed Constantinople. The Byzantine Morea (Peloponnese) fell in 1460, and Trebizond, a separate Greek empire, in 1461. Byzantium's legacy includes Justinian's law, the Orthodox Christianity of Bulgarians (864/865), Serbs and Rus' (988), and, through its copyists, most surviving ancient Greek literature.

Contested

  • The themes. The older view that Heraclius created them in one planned reform has few followers now. Treadgold (1995) argues that Constans II (641–668), Heraclius' grandson, created the first themes around 660, granting soldiers lands for their upkeep; Haldon (1999) sees the armies settling gradually, with lands tied to military service clearly attested only in the tenth century.
  • Manzikert. Older narratives call the battle of 1071 the disaster that lost Anatolia. Many historians now judge its losses fairly light, and Beihammer (2017) argues that Turkish groups gained ground less by conquest than by working their way into local politics, often as allies of rival Byzantine leaders in the revolts of 1071–1081.
  • Roman identity. Kaldellis (2019) argues that the Romans of Byzantium formed an ethnic group and a nation that absorbed many newcomers; the older view, still held by many, sees a multi-ethnic empire bound together by Orthodox Christianity and loyalty to the emperor.

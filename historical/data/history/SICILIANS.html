Sicilians

Who they were

The codex's Sicilians are the Italo-Normans and their kingdom, from Aversa, the first Norman lordship in Italy (1030), to King Manfred's death at Benevento (1266). Their principalities in southern Italy (Campania, Apulia, Calabria) and Sicily became, from 1130, the Kingdom of Sicily (Regnum Siciliae), which until 1160 also held part of coastal Ifriqiya (today's Tunisia and western Libya). "Sicilians" names that realm, not only the islanders. The Hautevilles ruled it until 1194, then the German Hohenstaufen. Their subjects were Christians who worshipped in Latin or Greek, Arabic-speaking Muslims, and Jews; the Normans, partly of Scandinavian descent, were French-speaking Christians.

Rise and height

From the early eleventh century Normans came south as mercenaries, among them the sons of Tancred of Hauteville; one, Robert Guiscard (roughly "the Cunning"), conquered Calabria. In 1059 Pope Nicholas II made Robert his vassal and duke of Apulia and Calabria, and of Sicily once conquered. Ibn al-Thumna, one of the rival commanders who divided Muslim Sicily, invited the Normans in. Robert and his youngest brother Roger took Messina (1061) and Palermo (1072); Roger I, now count of Sicily, ended the thirty-year conquest with Noto in 1091. Sicily stayed out of the First Crusade, but Robert's son Bohemond and his grandson, another Tancred, joined it; Bohemond made Antioch (1098) his principality. Count Roger's son Roger II, crowned at Palermo in 1130, built a sea power. His fleets, mostly under the Greek admiral George of Antioch, took Jerba (1135), Tripoli (1146) and Mahdia (1148) in Africa and sacked Thebes and Corinth in Greece (1147) (Houben 2002). When his grandson William II died childless (1189), Roger II's daughter Constance and her husband, Emperor Henry VI, won the crown by war in 1194; their son Frederick II reigned 1198–1250.

War and society

The Norman conquerors were few, probably a few thousand horsemen at most, and relied on Lombard (Latin south Italian), Greek and Muslim troops (Loud 2000; Metcalfe 2009). Like other Western knights, they won with massed cavalry charges, at Civitate (1053), Cerami (1063) and Dyrrhachium (1081), where Robert beat the Byzantine emperor Alexios I. Writing of Bohemond's war (1107–08), Alexios's daughter Anna Komnene said a mounted Frank could punch through the walls of Babylon. Castles of earth and timber, then stone keeps such as Adrano and Paternò, held the conquests. Serjeants (servientes), non-noble soldiers on foot or horseback, fought beside the knights. About 1150 the crown recorded how many knights and serjeants each fief (land held for military service) in Apulia and Capua owed to the general levy, the magna expeditio. Greek and Arab seamen manned the galleys; crusaders sailed east from the kingdom's ports.

Sicily grew rich on grain exports. In 1098 Pope Urban II made Roger I his legate (representative), giving Sicily's rulers rare control over their Church. A royal finance office on Arabic lines, the diwan, registered estates and their peasants, and the kings issued charters in Latin, Greek and Arabic (Johns 2002). Coexistence broke down: Christian rioters killed Muslims in Palermo in 1161, and after 1189 many Muslims rebelled in the mountains of western Sicily. Frederick II crushed them and, from about 1224, deported them to Lucera in Apulia, whose archers became a loyal royal corps (Taylor 2003).

End and legacy

The popes, the kingdom's feudal overlords, gave it to the French prince Charles of Anjou. Frederick's son Manfred, whose army included Lucera's archers, fell fighting him at Benevento in 1266. The kingdom endured, though after the Sicilian Vespers revolt (1282) the island passed to Aragon and Charles kept only the mainland. Charles II destroyed Lucera in 1300. Palermo's Arab-Norman buildings and the cathedrals of Cefalù and Monreale are a UNESCO World Heritage site.

Contested

  • How tolerant? Older and popular accounts celebrate Norman Sicily as a meeting of Latin, Greek and Arab cultures, and its kings did tolerate Greek Christians, Muslims and Jews and employ Greeks and Muslims at court (Houben 2002). More recent work stresses the limits: Metcalfe (2009) sees a pragmatic, unequal coexistence that broke down from the 1160s. Ibn Jubayr, a Muslim pilgrim from al-Andalus (Islamic Spain) visiting in 1184–85, saw both sides: Muslim courtiers, and Muslims fearful of pressure to convert.
  • The diwan. Earlier historians thought the Normans took over the Arabic offices of the Kalbid emirs, the island's tenth- and eleventh-century rulers; Johns (2002) argues that Roger II created a new diwan in the 1130s and 1140s on the model of Fatimid Egypt.
  • Numbers. Medieval chroniclers' figures, such as Malaterra's for Cerami, are often implausible, and modern estimates of the Norman forces are small (Loud 2000); how many Muslims went to Lucera, and how many archers it raised, is also uncertain (Taylor 2003).

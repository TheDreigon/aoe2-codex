Armenians

Who they were

From Ashot I's coronation (884 or 885) to the Byzantine annexation of Ani (1045) and, briefly, to its Seljuk capture (1064), the codex's Armenians are the people of Greater Armenia, a high plateau now mostly in Turkey and Armenia. The Bagratid kings' heartland was Shirak, around Ani; the Artsruni kings ruled Vaspurakan, around Lake Van; lesser kings and princes held Kars, Lori, Syunik and Taron. Armenians call themselves Hay, traditionally after Hayk, who, in a legend told by the historian Movses Khorenatsi, killed the tyrant Bel with an arrow. The kingdoms' roots lay in the Arsacid dynasty, of Parthian origin, which ended in 428, and in the nakharars, great noble houses that outlived it.

Rise and height

Divided between Byzantium and Persia, Armenia came mostly under the Arab caliphate from the mid-seventh century. After a great revolt was crushed (850–855), the caliph named the Bagratid Ashot "prince of princes" (862). In 884 or 885 he was crowned king with a crown sent by Caliph al-Mu'tamid (Garsoïan 1997); later Armenian historians add one from the Byzantine emperor Basil I. Yusuf, emir (governor) of Iranian Azerbaijan, recognised Gagik Artsruni as king of Vaspurakan (908) and put Ashot's son Smbat I (890–914) to death after his surrender. Smbat's son Ashot II, called Yerkat ("Iron"), won the kingdom back with Byzantine help. Ashot III (953–977) made Ani his capital in 961; his son Smbat II gave it new outer walls, and in about 992 the catholicos, head of the Armenian Church, moved there. Around 1000, under Gagik I, the kingdom reached its height.

War and society

Kings depended on the nakharars, who held lands, castles and hereditary offices, and on the azat ("free"), lesser nobles who rode in their service (Toumanoff 1963). Each house armed its own contingent from its lands, and the houses often fought one another or sided with outsiders. The army's commander, the sparapet, was long a Mamikonian; the office passed to the Bagratids and helped carry them to the throne. Fifth-century sources such as the Buzandaran ("Epic Histories") show noble horsemen armed mainly with lance and sword, occasionally with the bow (Garsoïan 1989). The twelfth-century chronicler Matthew of Edessa says that in the early 1040s the nobles, under the sparapet Vahram Pahlavuni, beat a Byzantine army sent to take Ani and had Gagik II crowned.

Caravans of pack animals between Byzantium and the Islamic world crossed Armenia's mountain roads. Tenth-century geographers describe Dvin, usually held by Muslim emirs, as a great market, and Armenian woollens and qirmiz, a red insect dye, were prized exports (Manandian 1965). The Armenian Church, founded in the early fourth century, later rejected the Council of Chalcedon (451) and its definition of Christ's nature, upheld by Byzantium. The Artsruni kings endowed Varag monastery, near Lake Van, home to a relic venerated as part of Christ's cross. Kings and nobles built churches faced with cut stone, often volcanic tuff, such as the island church of Aghtamar (915–921) and Ani's cathedral (989–1001). The chronicler Stepanos Taronetsi says its architect, Trdat, also planned the repair of Hagia Sophia's dome.

End and legacy

After the first Turkish raids, King Senekerim-Hovhannes of Vaspurakan ceded his kingdom to Byzantium (1021 or 1022) for lands around Sebasteia (Sivas). Basil II compelled Hovhannes-Smbat, king at Ani, to name him his heir (1022). Lured to Constantinople, Gagik II was forced to abdicate, and Ani was handed over in 1045. The Seljuk Turks sacked the merchant town of Artsn (1048–1049) and, under Sultan Alp Arslan, took Ani in 1064. A later Armenian state (to 1375) in Cilicia, on the Mediterranean coast, lies outside this civ. Ani's ruins, on the Turkish–Armenian border, became a World Heritage Site in 2016.

Contested

  • Bagratid armies. Tenth- and eleventh-century chroniclers count troops but rarely describe weapons, so the picture of the noble horseman rests mainly on fifth-century sources. Garsoïan (1989) judges the Military List (Zoranamak), which counts the horsemen each house owed the king, late and partly fictional; others read it as a record of the fourth-century Arsacid army.
  • Movses Khorenatsi. His History, the source of the Hayk legend, presents itself as fifth-century; Thomson (1978) argued that it was written centuries later, partly to glorify the Bagratid house, while many Armenian scholars defend the traditional date.
  • The conversion. Tradition dates King Trdat III's conversion by Gregory the Illuminator to 301, the basis of Armenia's claim to be the first Christian state; most modern scholars prefer about 314 (Garsoïan 1997).

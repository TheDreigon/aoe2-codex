Castilians

Who they were

The codex's Castilians are the kingdoms of León and Castile from 1037, when Ferdinand I, count of Castile, won León, to Isabella I's death in 1504. Twice divided between heirs (1065, 1157), they were joined for good in 1230 as the Crown of Castile. Their lands stretched from Galicia, Asturias and the Basque coast to Andalusia and Murcia. The name, Latin Castella ("castles"), first belonged to a fortified frontier district of the Asturian kingdom. The game calls them "Spanish", but medieval Hispania usually meant the whole peninsula; Castile and Aragon came under one royal couple only in 1479.

Rise and height

Bermudo III of León fell fighting Ferdinand I at Tamarón. Later, several taifas, small Muslim kingdoms born of the Caliphate of Córdoba's collapse, paid Ferdinand tribute (parias). Alfonso VI took Toledo in 1085, but the Almoravids, Berber rulers of Morocco, beat him at Sagrajas in 1086. Their successors, the Almohads, routed Alfonso VIII at Alarcos (1195); in 1212 he crushed them at Las Navas de Tolosa, with the kings of Aragon and Navarre (O'Callaghan 2003). Ferdinand III took Córdoba (1236), Jaén (1246) and Seville (1248), and the Nasrid emir of Granada became his tribute-paying vassal. Alfonso XI beat the Marinids of Morocco and their Granadan allies at the river Salado (1340). Isabella I (1474–1504) won a succession war against her niece Juana and Portugal (1475–1479). With her husband Ferdinand, king of Aragon from 1479, she conquered Granada (1482–1492).

War and society

Conquests were held by resettling them (repoblación): new towns received charters and local law codes (fueros). Cuenca's fuero (probably late twelfth century) made householders serve in the town militia. Commoners who kept a war horse, the caballeros villanos, won tax exemptions and town offices (Powers 1988). The military orders of Calatrava (1158), Santiago (1170) and Alcántara, knights under religious vows, held frontier castles and lordships. In 1348 Alfonso XI obliged men whose property reached a set value (cuantía) to keep horse and arms; such horsemen are best documented in Andalusia and Murcia. There, heavy cavalry fought beside jinetes, light horsemen with lance and javelins riding Berber-style with short stirrups. Crossbowmen were the main missile troops. In the war of Granada, royal artillery battered Ronda (1485), Loja (1486) and Málaga (1487) (Cook 1993). Indulgences (remission of penance for sins), sold under a papal crusade bull of 1482, helped pay for the war. The Santa Hermandad (1476), a crown-run league of the towns, policed the roads and raised men and money.

The Mesta, the sheep-owners' association recognised in 1273, drove flocks between northern summer and southern winter pastures. By the fifteenth century, merino wool, sold by Burgos merchants in Flanders, was the main export (Phillips and Phillips 1997); Basque iron was exported too, and Toledo blades were famous. Pilgrims from across Latin Europe visited the tomb at Compostela believed to hold the apostle James (Santiago), the kingdoms' patron saint. Jews and Mudéjars (Muslims under Christian rule) were protected but unequal: Alfonso X's law code, the Siete Partidas, forbade forcing Jews to convert but barred them from public office. Anti-Jewish massacres spread from Seville in 1391, forcing many to convert; the Inquisition (1478) tried converts accused of secretly practising Judaism.

End and legacy

Granada surrendered on 2 January 1492. That year Columbus, sailing for the monarchs, reached the Bahamas, Cuba and Hispaniola, lands joined to the Crown of Castile, and the Jews were ordered to convert or leave. In 1502 Castile's Muslims received the same order, on terms that made leaving nearly impossible. Isabella's grandson Charles came to rule Castile and Aragon (1516), which kept separate institutions until 1707–1716. Castilian, also called Spanish, spread across the Americas.

design notes and sources

Contested

  • "Reconquista" ("reconquest"). O'Callaghan (2003) keeps the term, since medieval kings already claimed to be recovering a lost Christian Spain. Ríos Saloma (2011) argues that it became a historians' category in the late eighteenth century and spread in the nineteenth with the idea of a Spanish nation; others call it misleading for a process of advances, truces and alliances.
  • "Convivencia". In a mid-twentieth-century debate, Américo Castro made the "living together" of Christians, Muslims and Jews the key to Spanish history, while Claudio Sánchez-Albornoz stressed roots older than the Muslim conquest and the struggle against Islam; Soifer (2009) argues that the concept now hinders more than it helps.
  • Guns and Granada. Cook (1993) argues that artillery decided the war; others stress the Nasrid civil war and Castile's far greater resources.

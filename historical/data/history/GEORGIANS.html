Georgians

Who they were

The codex's Georgians are the Kingdom of Georgia under the Bagrationi dynasty, from its union in 1008 to its division in 1490, with its core under David IV and Tamar (1089–1213). It lay in the southern Caucasus: Kartli around Tbilisi, Kakheti to the east, Imereti and Abkhazia to the west, Tao-Klarjeti in the south-west (now largely in Turkey) and the high valleys of Svaneti. Georgians call themselves Kartvelebi and their land Sakartvelo. Persians, Byzantines and Arabs had all ruled parts of it.

Rise and height

Bagrat III, king of Abkhazia (most of western Georgia) and ruler of Kartli, inherited his father's lands and royal title in 1008. From about 1080 Seljuk Turks overran much of the country, and George II paid them tribute. His son David IV (1089–1125), later called Aghmashenebeli, "the Builder", stopped the tribute in 1099. In about 1118 he brought the Kipchaks, his wife Gurandukht's Turkic steppe people, across the Caucasus. On 12 August 1121, at Didgori, west of Tbilisi, he routed a Muslim coalition under the emir Ilghazi; in 1122 he took Tbilisi, Muslim-ruled for most of four centuries, and made it his capital.

Tamar, co-ruler from 1178 and sole ruler in 1184–1213, was the first woman to rule Georgia in her own right. Her armies were led by her husband, David Soslan, an Alan (North Caucasian) prince, and two Armenian brothers, Zakare and Ivane Mkhargrdzeli (the Zakarids). They beat the Turkic rulers of Azerbaijan and Anatolia at Shamkor (1195) and Basiani (early 1200s), and took Ani (1199), Dvin and Kars (1206–1207). As Georgian vassals, the brothers rebuilt Armenian towns and monasteries. In 1204 her troops helped her kinsmen found the Empire of Trebizond, on the Black Sea.

War and society

Georgian armies fought mainly on horseback. The nobles (aznauri), from great lords to lesser knights, served with their own mounted followers. To depend less on the great lords, David IV formed a royal guard, the monaspa ("host of servants"). He also settled the Kipchak horse archers with their families (Golden 1984). At Didgori, says the Arab historian Ibn al-Athir, some 200 Kipchaks feigned surrender, then shot into the Muslim ranks and caused panic.

In about 1103 a church council at Ruisi and Urbnisi deposed bishops it judged unworthy. David also made the bishop of Chqondidi, the Chqondideli, his chancellor and chief minister (Lordkipanidze 1987). Georgia was Orthodox, like Byzantium; its monasteries were great landowners and centres of farming and learning. David founded Gelati monastery and academy in 1106; the Life of David calls it a second Jerusalem and another Athens. Pack animals carried timber, stone and ore over the passes. Tbilisi's caravan trade, with tribute and booty, paid for the golden age.

End and legacy

After Tamar, Mongol raids (1220–1221) began the decline. Jalal al-Din, the last shah of Central Asian Khwarazm, driven west by the Mongols, broke Georgian power at Garni (1225) and sacked Tbilisi (1226). The Mongols overran the east in 1236–1240; Queen Rusudan, holding the west, submitted in 1243. Mountain strongholds and high valleys such as Svaneti helped the dynasty and the Church survive. The west broke free of the Mongols in 1259–1260. George V the Brilliant reunited the realm by 1334; Mongol rule faded as the Mongol state in Iran broke up after 1335. Timur (Tamerlane) invaded repeatedly in 1386–1403. Turkoman (Turkic tribal) raids followed, but dynastic civil wars did most to split the kingdom: in 1490 a royal council accepted its division into the kingdoms of Kartli, Kakheti and Imereti and the principality of Samtskhe (Rayfield 2012). David IV and Tamar are Georgian saints, and Shota Rustaveli's epic The Knight in the Panther's Skin (c. 1200) is a national classic.

design notes and sources

Contested

  • The Kipchaks. The Life of David counts 40,000 chosen Kipchak fighting men, settled with their families. The usual reading, 40,000 families each owing a soldier (perhaps 160,000–200,000 people), is challenged by some Georgian historians, who propose far lower figures; Golden (1984) gathers the sources. That the Kipchaks decided Didgori is a modern reading of Ibn al-Athir, who wrote about a century later; Lordkipanidze (1987) holds that Georgian troops bore the brunt.
  • The monaspa. Modern overviews, Lordkipanidze (1987) among them, give the guard 5,000 horsemen, a figure of unclear documentary basis. The Life of David never uses the word; besides the Kipchak warriors, it mentions about 5,000 chosen servants (monani, as in monaspa), all made Christians. Whether the guard came from such men, from the Kipchaks or from lesser Georgian nobles is debated.
  • The Zakarids. Armenian chroniclers such as Kirakos Gandzaketsi present their conquests as a liberation of Armenia, Georgian chronicles as Georgian expansion; Zakare kept to the Armenian church, while Ivane joined the Georgian one.

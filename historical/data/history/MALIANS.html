Malians

Who they were

The codex's Malians are the Mali Empire of the Maninka (also Mandinka or Malinke), a Mande people of the upper Niger, under kings (mansa) of the Keita clan. "Mali", the Arabic writers' name, is probably a form of Manden, their homeland. At its height the empire reached from the Atlantic coast of Senegal and the Gambia to the desert-edge towns of Walata, Timbuktu and Gao. The civilization runs from Sunjata's victory at Kirina, conventionally dated 1235, to Mansa Mahmud IV's defeat outside Djenné in 1599, with its core in 1235–1400.

Rise and height

In 1068 the geographer al-Bakri described Malal, probably an early Mande kingdom, whose king had become a Muslim. Ghana, the Soninke kingdom to the north (not the modern country), later declined, and the Sosso rose to power; Mande epics call their king Sumaoro Kante. The epics tell how Sunjata Keita rallied the Maninka chiefs and defeated Sumaoro at Kirina (Niane 1960); the historian Ibn Khaldun calls the conqueror Mari Jata. His son Mansa Uli made the pilgrimage to Mecca while Baybars ruled Egypt (1260–1277). Under Mansa Musa (reigned 1312–1337 or 1307–1332) Mali held Timbuktu and Gao. On pilgrimage in 1324 he spent so much gold in Cairo that, the historian al-Umari reported, gold was still cheaper there about twelve years later. The Catalan Atlas (c. 1375) shows "Musse Melly" enthroned with a golden orb. Musa's brother Sulayman received the Moroccan traveller Ibn Battuta in 1352–1353.

War and society

Al-Umari's informants put the army at about 100,000 men, 10,000 of them horsemen (Levtzion and Hopkins 1981); most were probably called up from the provinces. The horsemen, nobles with lance, javelins and sword, rode costly horses, many brought across the Sahara (Law 1980). The foot soldiers were spearmen and archers (Levtzion 1973); Ibn Battuta saw the mansa hold court carrying a bow and quiver. Mande tradition remembers Sunjata's warriors as the sixteen clans of "quiver-bearers" (tontajon). Near the empire's western edge, poisoned arrows killed the Portuguese captain Nuno Tristão and most of his crew about 1446 (Zurara c. 1453). In 1455 archers in canoes attacked the Venetian Cadamosto's ships on the Gambia, whose chiefs, he was told in 1456, obeyed the "emperor of Melli".

Mali's wealth came from the goldfields of Bambuk and Bure (Levtzion 1973). Caravans traded the gold for Saharan salt, used as money south of the desert; at the Taghaza salt mines Ibn Battuta saw houses built of salt. The mansas ruled subject towns through governors called farba, which Ibn Battuta translates as "deputy". Builders used banco (sun-dried earth) and little timber, as in Timbuktu's great mosque, the Djinguereber, credited to Musa (c. 1327) and much rebuilt since. The court was Muslim: Ibn Battuta saw children fettered until they knew the Qur'an by heart, though poets in bird costumes performed a custom said to predate Islam. Mande Muslim traders and clerics, the Wangara or Juula, spread trade and Islam to Hausaland (now northern Nigeria) and the southern forest edge.

End and legacy

Ibn Khaldun reports disputed successions and ministers who seized power after Sulayman's death in 1360. Mali lost Timbuktu to the Tuareg, Saharan nomads, about 1433; Songhay, the kingdom of Gao, took the town in 1468–69, and Timbuktu's scholarly peak came under its rule (Hunwick 1999). In 1599 Moroccan musketeers, victors over Songhay at Tondibi in 1591, routed Mansa Mahmud IV's army outside Djenné, and Mali broke into Mande chiefdoms. Griots, hereditary bards, still recite Sunjata's epic, and in 1960 the Republic of Mali took the empire's name.

Contested

  • Sunjata and Kirina. Ibn Khaldun names the conqueror only as Mari Jata. Sunjata's story, Kirina and the Kurukan Fuga assembly (said to have assigned the clans their roles) come from griots' epics written down only in modern times, most famously by Niane (1960). The date 1235 is a modern estimate, and how far the epics preserve thirteenth-century institutions, such as the tontajon clans, is debated.
  • The capital. The historian Niane (1960) and many others placed Mali's capital at the site of Niani, in today's Guinea; Fauvelle (2013) and others reject this and consider the capital unidentified.
  • The Atlantic voyage. Musa told a Cairo official that his predecessor had sailed into the Atlantic with a great fleet and never returned (al-Umari, in Levtzion and Hopkins 1981). Fauvelle (2013) stresses that Musa told the story to explain his own accession; a few writers claim the fleet reached the Americas, which no archaeological evidence supports.

Turks

Who they were

The codex's Turks are the Turkish sultanates of Anatolia and the Balkans: the Seljuk sultanate of Rum ("Rome", the Muslim name for Byzantine lands) and, above all, the Ottoman state, named after its founder Osman. They run from Manzikert (1071) to Mohács (1526), with an Ottoman core in 1389–1500. Rum's rulers were a branch of the Seljuks, Muslim Oghuz Turks; their Great Seljuk state in Iran belongs to the codex's Persians. The Ottomans called themselves Osmanlı; Europeans called both Turks, a name first borne by the Türks, whose steppe empire arose in Inner Asia in 552.

Rise and height

At Manzikert the Great Seljuk sultan Alp Arslan defeated and captured Emperor Romanos IV on 26 August 1071. Amid Byzantine civil wars, Turkmen (Muslim Turkish nomads) spread across Anatolia, and the Seljuk prince Süleyman founded a state at Nicaea. After the First Crusade helped Byzantium recover Nicaea (1097), his heirs ruled from Konya. They won the ports of Antalya in 1207 and Sinop in 1214 (Ibn Bibi 1280s). Rum was at its height under Kayqubad I (1220–1237). The Mongols crushed its army at Köse Dağ (1243); the dynasty, now their vassal, died out around 1308.

As Rum declined, Osman's beylik (principality) grew on the Byzantine frontier in north-western Anatolia. His son Orhan took Bursa (1326) and aided the rival Byzantine emperor John VI Kantakouzenos, whose daughter Theodora he married (1346); by 1354 the Ottomans held Gallipoli, in Europe. Murad I took Adrianople (Edirne) and died at the battle of Kosovo (1389). Bayezid I crushed a crusade at Nicopolis (1396) but was captured by Timur (Tamerlane) at Ankara (1402); civil war followed until 1413. Murad II beat another crusade at Varna (1444), and on 29 May 1453 Mehmed II took Constantinople (today's Istanbul) and made it his capital (Kritovoulos c. 1467). By 1500 the sultans ruled most of the Balkans and Anatolia.

War and society

Seljuk armies relied on Turkmen horse archers, who usually kept their distance until an enemy's ranks broke. At Manzikert they gave way before the emperor's advance and struck when he turned back and his rearguard withdrew (Attaleiates c. 1080). Byzantine emperors hired such horsemen from the 1070s; crusaders raised Turcopoles who fought in the Turkish style. Rum's sultans, whose court wrote in Persian, ruled Greek and Armenian Christians and Turkmen herders; the mystic poet Rumi (died 1273) taught in Konya. The sultans founded hospitals, such as Kayseri's (1205–1206), and built royal hans (caravanserais): fortified inns for merchants, a day's march apart on the roads from Konya, Kayseri and Sivas to their ports (Cahen 1968).

Ottoman provincial cavalrymen (sipahis), armed with bow, lance and mace, held a timar, the revenue of assigned villages, and brought retainers in proportion to it (İnalcık 1973). The sultan's salaried household troops included the Janissaries (yeniçeri, "new troops"). Murad I formed them from his one-in-five share of war captives; by the 1390s they also came from the devşirme, a levy of Christian boys raised as Muslims. Archers at first, they took up firearms from the mid-fifteenth century. The akıncı ("raiders"), light horsemen led by frontier families such as the Mihaloğlu, struck as far as north-eastern Italy (1477, 1499). Mehmed II hired gun-founders such as Orban for great bombards and after 1453 set up a cannon foundry in Istanbul (Ágoston 2005). In 1499 a fleet from the arsenals (tersane) of Gallipoli and Istanbul beat the Venetians off Zonchio.

End and legacy

Selim I (1512–1520) routed Iran's Safavid shah Ismail at Chaldiran (1514), where Ottoman guns and Janissaries fought behind a barrier of carts, and conquered Mamluk Syria and Egypt (1516–1517). Süleyman I destroyed the Hungarian army at Mohács (1526). The empire lasted until 1922, and many Seljuk hans still stand.

design notes and sources

Contested

  • The gazi thesis. Wittek (1938) explained the Ottoman rise by the zeal of frontier warriors for holy war (gaza) against their Christian neighbours. Critics point to Osman's Christian allies, to tribal and material motives and to chronicles written long after the events; Kafadar (1995) keeps gaza as one strand of a fluid frontier culture.
  • The timar's origin. İnalcık (1973) traced the timar mainly to the iqta', a grant of revenue for military service used by earlier Islamic states, the Seljuks among them. He also showed that holders of the pronoia, a similar Byzantine grant, entered the early Ottoman registers in the Balkans. Some Byzantinists have seen the timar as a continuation of the pronoia.

Bulgarians

Who they were

The codex's Bulgarians are the two medieval Bulgarian empires of the Balkans. The First (681–1018), a steppe-style khanate, became a tsardom (empire) under Simeon; after Byzantine rule came the Second (1185–1396), ruled by the Asen and later Shishman dynasties. Their heartland was the lower Danube and the Balkan Mountains; at times they held much of Thrace and Macedonia. The founding Bulgars, steppe herders from north of the Black Sea, ruled the Slavs, already settled south of the Danube in their own chiefdoms; the two merged into one Slavic-speaking people. The Second Empire drew on Vlachs (many of them herders, speaking a Latin-based language) and Cuman allies (Turkic nomads).

Rise and height

In 680 Asparuh's Bulgars defeated Emperor Constantine IV's army near the Danube delta; by the peace conventionally dated 681, Byzantium paid them tribute. Khan Krum (c. 803–814) defeated and killed Emperor Nikephoros I (811) and took Mesembria and Adrianople (812–813). Boris I accepted Byzantine baptism in 864 or 865 and in 886 welcomed disciples of Cyril and Methodius, whose Moravian mission produced the first Slavic alphabet, Glagolitic. Simeon (893–927) won yearly tribute after defeating Byzantium at Bulgarophygon (896), took the title of tsar and in 924 annexed Serbia (Fine 1983). In 927 Byzantium recognised his son Peter as tsar and resumed payments.

In 1185 the brothers Peter and Asen led the Vlachs and Bulgarians of the Balkan Mountains in revolt against a tax for Emperor Isaac II's wedding, founding the Second Empire. Kaloyan (1197–1207) fought the crusaders' Latin Empire of Constantinople. At Adrianople on 14 April 1205 his Cuman horse archers lured its knights into a pursuit; he routed them and captured Emperor Baldwin I. Ivan Asen II (1218–1241) defeated Epirus at Klokotnitsa (1230) and in 1235 won recognition of a Bulgarian patriarch (Madgearu 2016).

War and society

The Bulgar elite kept steppe titles such as boila, but wrote its inscriptions mostly in Greek; some list mail coats and helmets issued from the khan's stores (Beševliev 1963). Armoured nobles, the boilas and later bolyars, formed the army's mounted elite, beside Slav foot soldiers with spears, javelins and bows. Steppe horse archery probably came with the Bulgars, but Balkan evidence of it is thin before the Cumans.

Great victories came in mountain passes. In 811, the Byzantine chronicler Theophanes reports, Krum trapped Nikephoros in a pass closed with palisades. Samuel, the Bulgarian commander, ambushed Emperor Basil II at the Gates of Trajan (986), and the Asens struck Isaac II's army from the heights near Tryavna (1190). Theophanes adds that an Arab engineer, a Byzantine deserter, taught Krum's men to build siege engines. Kaloyan took Varna (1201) with a siege tower, and hill fortresses such as Tsarevets at Tarnovo, the capital, anchored the Second Empire.

Herding mattered in the highlands: around 1075 the Byzantine writer Kekaumenos described Thessaly's Vlachs keeping their flocks and families in high mountains from April to September. In 893, historians reconstruct, an assembly now called the Council of Preslav enthroned Simeon, moved the capital to Preslav and replaced Greek with Slavonic in the church. A second alphabet, Cyrillic, took shape, probably in Preslav, and Slavonic books from Preslav, Ohrid and later Tarnovo passed to Serbs and Rus' (Obolensky 1971).

End and legacy

The peace of 927 ended when Emperor Nikephoros II stopped payments (965/966): Sviatoslav of Kiev invaded, and in 971 Emperor John I Tzimiskes took Preslav and eastern Bulgaria. Samuel held western Bulgaria until Basil II defeated his army at Kleidion (1014); Bulgaria submitted in 1018 (Stephenson 2000). After 1242 the Second Empire paid tribute to the Mongols and later split between Tarnovo and Vidin (Fine 1987). The Ottomans took Tarnovo in 1393 and Vidin in 1396. Cyrillic is still written from the Balkans to the Pacific.

design notes and sources

Contested

  • Bulgar origins. Most scholars class the Bulgars' language, known from titles, names and a few words, as Oghuric Turkic, a branch whose only living language is Chuvash, spoken on the Volga; an Iranian origin, popular in Bulgaria, remains a minority view.
  • The Asens. Chroniclers of the time call Peter and Asen Vlachs. Vásáry (2005) reads Asen's Turkic names and the brothers' Cuman ties as signs of steppe descent, while Madgearu (2016) thinks they probably had Bulgarian, Vlach and Cuman forebears; Bulgarian and Romanian historians have long disputed whether the revolt was mainly Bulgarian or Vlach.
  • Kleidion. Byzantine sources say Basil II blinded 14,000 or 15,000 prisoners; some historians think the figure inflated, and Stephenson (2003) argues that his fame as the "Bulgar-slayer" took shape only in the late twelfth century.

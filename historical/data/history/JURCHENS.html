Jurchens

Who they were

The Jurchens (Chinese Nüzhen), whose language developed into Manchu, were a people of the forests and river valleys of Manchuria, not steppe nomads. The Khitan Liao empire, a steppe power, ruled the southern Jurchens directly; the northern ones kept their own chiefs, and the Wanyan clan of the Anchuhu ("Gold") River, a tributary of the Songhua, came to lead them. The codex covers 1114 to 1234: the Wanyan rising and the Jin ("Gold") dynasty, probably named after the Anchuhu. From 1127 they conquered north China, ruling it partly through the puppet states of Chu and Qi until 1137; from 1141–42 the Huai River was their frontier with Song China.

Rise and height

Resenting Liao demands, among them hunting falcons and pearls, the Wanyan chief Aguda rose in 1114, won at Ningjiang that autumn and proclaimed the Jin early in 1115. His cavalry armies routed Liao forces said to be far larger and, by his death in 1123, had taken all five Liao capitals. His Song allies of 1120 failed in 1122 to take Yanjing (Beijing), and the partners fell out over payments, land and a defector. Aguda's brother and successor Wuqimai captured the last Liao emperor in 1125 and invaded the Song that winter. Kaifeng, the Song capital, fell in January 1127; two emperors, thousands of courtiers and craftsmen, and books and astronomical instruments were taken north (the Jingkang incident). The Song fought on from the south until the peace of 1141–42 brought the Jin yearly payments of silver and silk (Franke 1994). Wanyan Liang (1150–1161) moved the capital to Yanjing, renamed Zhongdu, in 1153; his invasion of 1161 failed, and his officers killed him. Shizong (1161–1189) and Zhangzong (1189–1208) presided over what is usually seen as the Jin's heyday.

War and society

The Jurchens hunted, fished, farmed and kept pigs, horses and cattle; great court hunts trained the horsemen. In 1114 Aguda grouped his people into mouke of 300 households and meng'an of ten mouke. Each was at once community, tax unit and regiment, its households arming and feeding their own soldiers (Tao 1976). After the conquest these units were settled on allotted land across north China; by 1183 their households counted some six million people, slaves included, a minority among the Han Chinese. The court governed largely in Chinese style but created a Jurchen script (1119); shamanism endured beside Buddhism and Daoism.

Song reports describe early Jin units with heavily armoured lancers in front and archers behind (Xu Mengxin 1194); in 1140 the Song called the armoured guard of Aguda's son Wuzhu the "iron pagoda" (tie futu). Liao and Song engineers gave the Jin siegecraft: batteries of man-powered trebuchets pounded Taiyuan and Kaifeng, both taken in 1126–27. Han conscript infantry and Khitan troops later swelled the army. Against the steppe, mostly after 1150, the Jin dug the jiehao ("boundary trenches"), long lines of ditch and rampart with forts. Besieging the Song city of Qizhou in 1221, they threw iron-cased gunpowder bombs from trebuchets; in 1232 Kaifeng's defenders lowered such "heaven-shaking thunder" bombs (zhentianlei) on chains onto Mongols undermining the walls, and used fire lances, gunpowder tubes on spears (Andrade 2016).

End and legacy

From 1211 the Mongols crossed the jiehao and routed the Jin armies; the court withdrew to Kaifeng in 1214, and Zhongdu fell in 1215. Many Jin officers and engineers went over to the Mongols. Emperor Aizong fled besieged Kaifeng, which surrendered in 1233, and killed himself when Mongol and Song troops stormed Caizhou on 9 February 1234. In 1616 the Jurchen chief Nurhaci founded a Later Jin, whose people took the name Manchu in 1635.

design notes and sources

Contested

  • The Iron Pagoda. Song accounts of the battles of 1140 describe Wuzhu's armoured guard as men linked in threes by leather thongs. In the eighteenth century the Qing dynasty's Qianlong emperor dismissed this as impractical for horsemen. Historians still debate whether these troops fought mounted, and whether guaizi ma, the Song name for the linked troops at Yancheng, meant chained horses or flanking cavalry.
  • Gunpowder. Andrade (2016) reads accounts of the second siege of Kaifeng (1126–27) as showing that the Jin, using captured Song craftsmen, already threw gunpowder bombs; iron-cased bombs, far more powerful than the older soft-shelled kind, are first recorded in 1221. The "flying fire" of 1232, often called the first war rockets in popular histories, is a "flying fire lance" in the Jin shi, the dynasty's official history; when true rockets appeared is still debated (Needham 1986).

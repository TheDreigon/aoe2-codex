Danes

Who they were

Sixth-century Greek and Latin writers name the Danes. The codex follows their kingdom from 808, when King Godfred ordered its southern border fortified, to 1500. It closes with the end of the Kalmar Union of Denmark, Norway and Sweden in 1523. Its heartland was Jutland, the Danish islands and, across the Sound (Øresund), Scania, Halland and Blekinge, now in southern Sweden. Danish power also reached the Danelaw, the Danish-ruled part of England (9th–10th centuries), Cnut's North Sea empire (1016–1035) and Baltic conquests from Rügen to Estonia (1168–1346).

Rise and height

In 808, the Royal Frankish Annals report, Godfred attacked the Slavic Obotrites, destroyed the trading place of Reric, moved its merchants to Hedeby and ordered work on the Danevirke. This rampart across the neck of Jutland grew in phases from about 500 to the twelfth century. Danish fleets raided Frankish lands and England for decades. In 865 what the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle calls a "great army" landed in England; it seized Northumbria, East Anglia and eastern Mercia, where its men settled. By 954 English kings had conquered the Danelaw.

At Jelling, King Gorm raised a runestone for his wife Thyra. His son Harald Bluetooth, baptised around 965, raised a larger one proclaiming he had won all Denmark and Norway and made the Danes Christian (Roesdahl 1982). Harald's son Sweyn Forkbeard rebelled against him around 986 and conquered England in 1013, dying early in 1014. Sweyn's son Cnut gained England in 1016, then Denmark and, in 1028, Norway; in 1027 he claimed to rule only "part of the Swedes" (Bolton 2017). His sons held England until 1042.

Valdemar I (1157–1182) and Bishop Absalon crusaded against the Wends, pagan Slavs of the southern Baltic, taking Arkona on Rügen in 1168. Valdemar II (1202–1241) crusaded in Estonia, winning at Lyndanisse (Tallinn) in 1219, but defeat at Bornhöved (1227) sealed the loss of his north German conquests (Jensen 2017). In 1397 Margaret I formed the Kalmar Union under her great-nephew Erik of Pomerania. From about 1429 Erik levied a toll on ships passing the Sound at Helsingør.

War and society

Viking-age armies travelled by ship and on horseback but fought on foot. They took towns mostly by surprise; Paris withstood their long siege (885–886). Plunder and tribute paid for kings and war bands: England paid 10,000 pounds of silver in 991 and 48,000 in 1012. Hedeby's merchants dealt in slaves, furs and silver. Under Harald, around 975–980, ring forts such as Trelleborg were built to one geometric plan (Goodchild et al. 2017). In England Cnut kept household troops, the housecarls (húskarlar, "house-men"). Sagas of about 1200 tell of the Jomsvikings, a sworn brotherhood at Jomsborg. They place its defeat at Hjörungavágr in Norway (c. 986), a battle that contemporary court poets (skalds) mention.

Lund became the archbishopric of all Scandinavia in 1103/04. In the 1130s, the historian Saxo reports, a royal claimant still needed Germans to build throwing engines. From about 1200 the autumn herring of the Sound drew merchants, many of them German, to the Scania Market at Skanör and Falsterbo, whose dues enriched the crown. Kings now relied increasingly on armoured horsemen, lords exempted from the naval levy (leding) in return; crossbows defended castles and ships. King Hans's carvel-built (smooth-planked) flagship Gribshunden carried guns when she sank in 1495. In 1500 the peasant republic of Dithmarschen flooded the marsh at Hemmingstedt and routed his invading army.

End and legacy

Sweden broke away for good after King Christian II executed his opponents in the Stockholm Bloodbath (1520). In 1523 the Swedes elected Gustav Vasa king, and Danish nobles deposed Christian. Denmark and Norway stayed united until 1814, and ships paid the Sound toll until 1857. Place-names in -by and -thorpe still mark the Danelaw.

design notes and sources

Contested

  • The ring forts. Once dated to about 1000 and read as barracks for the conquest of England, they are now dated to Harald Bluetooth's reign. Some scholars see a project soon abandoned and mainly symbolic; Goodchild et al. (2017) argue that the forts formed an effective system of regional defence.
  • The Jomsvikings. No contemporary source names them or Jomsborg; the sagas disagree on who founded the brotherhood (Harald Bluetooth or the chieftain Pálnatóki), and Jomsborg's usual identification with Wolin, at the mouth of the Oder, is not certain. Some historians see a real war band behind the stories; others, a literary creation.
  • The housecarls. An older view, resting largely on a Danish account of Cnut's household law written in the 1180s, saw a disciplined, paid standing guard; Hooper (1985) argues that they were retainers rewarded with land, pay or both, hard to tell apart from thegns (English landholding nobles).

Chinese

Who they were

The codex's Chinese represent China under the Sui, Tang, Five Dynasties, Song and early Ming (581–1500), centred on the Song (960–1279) and early Ming (1368–c. 1450). Their land was China proper: the Yellow River and Yangtze basins and the south. From the Mongol Yuan (1271–1368) the codex keeps only what the Ming inherited, such as metal guns.

Rise and height

Yang Jian, founder of the Sui, conquered the southern Chen in 589, reuniting China. The Sui built the Grand Canal (605–610) and long walls against the Türks, steppe nomads, but failed wars against Goguryeo, a Korean kingdom, brought revolt. In 618 Li Yuan founded the Tang, whose tax census of 754 registered about 53 million people. After An Lushan's revolt (755–763) military governors grew powerful; the Tang fell in 907. In 960 the army of the Later Zhou, last of five short-lived northern dynasties, made its general Zhao Kuangyin emperor; by 979 his Song dynasty had absorbed the rival kingdoms, but the Khitan Liao kept the far north around modern Beijing. At Chanyuan a Song bed crossbow (a heavy frame-mounted crossbow) killed a Liao commander in 1004; peace in 1005 cost the Song yearly payments. In 1127 the Jurchen Jin took Kaifeng and the north; the Song held the south until the Mongols broke through at Xiangyang (1273) and destroyed the last Song fleet (1279). The rebel Zhu Yuanzhang founded the Ming (1368); his son, the Yongle emperor (r. 1402–1424), launched Zheng He's Indian Ocean voyages (1405–1433).

War and society

Tang armies leaned on frontier cavalry; the Song, cut off from the northern pastures, lacked horses and kept huge standing infantry armies. Their main missile weapon was the heavy crossbow, which the military manual Wujing zongyao (1044) calls China's strong weapon, feared by its neighbours. At Heshangyuan (1131) Wu Jie's archers and crossbowmen shot in relays and held off the Jin. The pao, a traction trebuchet worked by teams pulling ropes, was China's main siege engine, still used after Muslim engineers built counterweight trebuchets for the Mongols at Xiangyang.

The Wujing zongyao also gives the earliest known gunpowder formulas; the fire lance, a gunpowder tube on a spear, is first described in 1132, and rockets followed, at a debated date. Tower ships (louchuan) had served Chinese fleets since the Han; at Caishi (1161) Song paddle-wheel warships hurling bombs helped stop a Jin crossing of the Yangtze (Needham 1986), and at Lake Poyang (1363) Zhu Yuanzhang's fire ships burned much of his rival's fleet. The oldest securely dated gun was cast in 1298, under the Yuan; early Ming regulations called for one soldier in ten to carry a gun, and Yongle formed a firearms division (Andrade 2016). By rule, Ming frontier garrisons farmed state land (tuntian), seven soldiers farming for every three on guard.

Under the Song, Confucian examinations became the most prestigious road into a civil service governing from county seats. Elvin (1973) speaks of a medieval economic revolution: the court spread early-ripening Champa rice (1012), guilds (hang) ran city trades, the government issued paper money (1024), Bi Sheng invented movable type (c. 1040) and sailors steered by compass. Buddhism and Daoism flourished; Arab and Persian merchants settled in the ports.

End and legacy

Around 1500 the Ming stood on the defensive in the north: after Oirat Mongols captured the emperor at Tumu (1449), the Ming built walls along the Ordos frontier (1474); the brick Great Wall is mostly later. A ban on private sea trade (1371–1567) confined legal foreign trade to tribute missions; smuggling persisted. Korea (958) and Đại Việt (Vietnam, 1075) had adopted Chinese-style examinations, and gunpowder spread across Eurasia.

design notes and sources

Contested

  • The Song "economic revolution". Elvin (1973) saw revolutionary change in Song farming, transport, money and technology; Bray (1984) finds that double cropping (two harvests a year), often credited to Champa rice, spread beyond the Lower Yangtze only under the Ming and Qing.
  • The Great Wall. Popular accounts present one wall built and rebuilt over two thousand years; Waldron (1990) argues that this single wall is a modern idea: the Tang and Song built no long walls, and the brick wall of the photographs is mostly sixteenth-century Ming.
  • The Ming guns. The Ming shi says that Yongle founded his firearms division to drill with "divine-engine" guns whose method was obtained in the conquest of Đại Việt (1407); Sun (2003) argues that the main flow of gun technology ran from China to Đại Việt.

Anglo-Saxons

Who they were

The Anglo-Saxons were the English-speaking peoples of lowland Britain from 449 to the Norman Conquest of 1066 (core period 871–1066), first in several kingdoms, then in one. The monk Bede (731) traced them to three continental peoples: Angles, Saxons and Jutes. His date of 449 is traditional: Saxons were raiding Roman Britain by the fourth century, and settlement probably began earlier. In time they called themselves English (Englisc), after the Angles, whom the game's "Saxons" leaves out. They held most of today's England and, for a time, Lothian in south-east Scotland.

Rise and height

Kingdoms formed in the sixth and seventh centuries, among them Kent, Wessex, Mercia, Northumbria and East Anglia. Missionaries from Rome reached Kent in 597, and Irish monks from Iona helped convert Northumbria, home of Bede and the Lindisfarne Gospels. Offa of Mercia (757–796) dominated southern England; Alfred's biographer Asser credits him with the dyke on the Welsh border. Viking raids began around 789, and from 865 a "great army" overran Northumbria, East Anglia and much of Mercia. Alfred of Wessex (871–899) defeated Guthrum's Danes at Edington in 878; in 886, the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle says, he occupied London and all the English not under Danish rule submitted to him. His children Edward the Elder (899–924) and Æthelflæd, Lady of the Mercians (d. 918), conquered the Danish-held lands south of the Humber. Æthelstan (924–939) took York in 927, becoming the first king of all the English, and beat the Scots and Dublin Norse at Brunanburh (937). Edgar (959–975) reigned without major Viking attacks.

War and society

Land was assessed in hides, in theory one household's land. Hides fixed military service (Abels 1988) and food payments (feorm): in the laws of King Ine of Wessex (c. 694), ten hides owed honey, ale, cattle, cheeses, eels and more. The Burghal Hidage, an early tenth-century list, assigns hides to some thirty burhs (fortified places): one man per hide, four men per pole (about 5 metres) of rampart (Hill and Rumble 1996). Most had earth banks and timber defences, though Winchester and Exeter reused Roman walls. In 893 Alfred split his army (fyrd) so that half served while half stayed home. In 896 he had ships built nearly twice as long as the Danes'.

The English rode to war but usually fought on foot, in a shield wall (bordweall), an old Germanic and late Roman formation, not a Viking import. Lords fought amid their household troop (heorðwerod); in the poem on the battle of Maldon (991), Byrhtnoth's loyal men die beside him. From 690, English missions led by Willibrord and later Boniface (d. 754) organised the Church in Frisia and much of Germany. At home the Danish settlers converted, and Dunstan and Æthelwold reformed the monasteries. Edgar's coinage reform (c. 973) gave the kingdom a uniform silver penny, struck at burh mints and repeatedly reissued in new designs (Molyneaux 2015).

End and legacy

Raids resumed under Æthelred II (978–1016), who from 991 paid ever larger tributes, raised by a land tax (the geld). The Dane Cnut won the throne in 1016, and his dynasty held it until 1042. Edward the Confessor (1042–1066), half-Norman and long exiled in Normandy, died childless on 5 January 1066; Earl Harold Godwinson, whom English sources say Edward named successor, was crowned the next day. Harold crushed a Norwegian invasion at Stamford Bridge but fell at Hastings on 14 October to William of Normandy, who claimed Edward had promised him the crown. English revolts lasted until 1071, yet the Normans retained the English shires (counties), coinage and geld.

design notes and sources

Contested

  • The name. "Anglo-Saxon" began as a continental name for the Saxons of Britain, and Asser called Alfred "king of the Anglo-Saxons" (Keynes and Lapidge 1983). Citing its racialised modern uses, some scholars now prefer "early medieval English", and in 2019 the International Society of Anglo-Saxonists voted to change its name; many others still use the term.
  • The migration. Views have ranged from a small warrior elite ruling Britons to mass migration. Ancient DNA points to large-scale migration: early medieval people sampled in eastern England drew up to three-quarters of their ancestry from across the North Sea, with strong regional variation and mixing with the Britons (Gretzinger et al. 2022).
  • The late kingdom. James Campbell's "maximum view" sees an unusually strong, centralised state (Campbell 2000); others qualify it, and George Molyneaux dates most of its machinery to Edgar's reign and stresses how loosely the north was held (Molyneaux 2015).

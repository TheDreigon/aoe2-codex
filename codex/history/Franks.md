# Franks

## Who they were

The codex's Franks (481–1500) cover two linked realms. First came the Frankish realm of the Merovingian (481–751) and Carolingian (751–987) kings, then the main focus: France under the Capetians (from 987) and their Valois branch (from 1328). Roman writers first name the Franks, a confederation of peoples on the lower Rhine, in the third century. Their kings came to rule most of Gaul, with a heartland between the Loire and the Rhine. After the realm's three-way split in 843, the codex follows the western kingdom, the future France; the eastern one is the root of the codex's Germans. Historians usually speak of the French after 987; the codex keeps the game's name, which crusading-era Byzantines and Muslims commonly gave western Europeans (Greek *Frangoi*, Arabic *Ifranj*).

## Rise and height

Clovis (481–511) defeated Syagrius, the Roman ruler at Soissons, in 486 and the Visigoths at Vouillé in 507. His baptism as a Catholic, the faith of his Gallo-Roman subjects, helped win him the bishops' support; its date (496–508) is debated (Wood 1994). From the late seventh century power shifted towards the kings' chief officers, the mayors of the palace. The mayor Charles Martel defeated a Muslim army from Spain near Poitiers in 732 or 733, his men fighting on foot, as a chronicle of 754 suggests (Fouracre 2000). His son Pippin became king in 751. Charlemagne (768–814) conquered the Lombard kingdom and Saxony, crushed the Avars and was crowned emperor in Rome in 800.

Viking raids and powerful regional lords weakened the western kings. Hugh Capet, chosen king in 987, came from the Robertian family, which had already produced two kings (Dunbabin 2000). Philip Augustus (1180–1223) took Normandy from John of England (1204) and won at Bouvines (1214); Louis IX (1226–1270) led two crusades. The Hundred Years War (1337–1453) began over Gascony and Edward III of England's claim to the French throne. After French defeats at Crécy (1346) and Agincourt (1415), Joan of Arc helped lift the siege of Orléans and led Charles VII to his coronation at Reims (1429).

## War and society

Early Frankish armies fought largely on foot. Procopius describes the Franks who invaded Italy in 539 hurling their axes together at the first charge; Isidore of Seville says people in Spain called axes *franciscas* because the Franks used them. Heavy cavalry grew gradually: in 805 a capitulary (royal decree) demanded a mail shirt from every man holding twelve *mansi* (peasant farms).

Wealth came from great estates worked by dependent peasants. Abbot Irminon's survey of Saint-Germain-des-Prés, near Paris (c. 823–829), lists tenants, dues and water mills; later, peasants had to use the lord's mill. From the late tenth century castles multiplied, including early stone keeps such as Loches. Eleventh-century knights learned to charge with the lance held tight under the arm. Knightly armour grew by stages, from mail to full plate by about 1420. Abbot Suger's choir (the church's east end) at Saint-Denis (1140–44) helped launch Gothic architecture, which a German chronicler of about 1280 called "French work" (*opus francigenum*). The University of Paris, given royal privileges in 1200, drew students from across Catholic Europe.

In 1445 Charles VII organised fifteen companies of 100 "lances" (a man-at-arms, an armoured horseman, with his mounted followers), paid from a yearly tax, the *taille*: France's first permanent royal army (Contamine 1972). The royal artillery, run by the brothers Jean and Gaspard Bureau, helped retake Normandy (1449–50) and broke the English attack at Castillon (1453).

## End and legacy

After 1453 England held only Calais in France. Louis XI gained the duchy of Burgundy (1477) and Provence (1481); Charles VIII married Anne, duchess of Brittany, in 1491 and invaded Italy in 1494, opening the Italian Wars. The Frankish realm had several heirs, and Charlemagne was claimed on both sides of the Rhine.

The design reads the estates and mills into *Mill technologies free*, the castles into *Castles cost -15/25% in Castle/Imperial Age*, knightly armour into *Mounted Units +20% HP starting in Feudal Age* and Gothic into *Masonry, Architecture free*; the University of Paris gives the team bonus, *Universities work +80% faster*. ## Contested

- **Charles Martel's cavalry.** In 1887 Heinrich Brunner argued that Charles Martel seized Church lands to pay for a cavalry army against the Arabs, and that this began feudalism; in 1962 Lynn White made the stirrup the trigger. Since Bernard Bachrach's critique of 1970, most historians reject both and see heavy cavalry growing gradually (Fouracre 2000).
- **Poitiers.** Most historians date the battle to 732, some to 733. Edward Gibbon and many after him saw it as saving Europe from Islam; James Palmer argues that contemporaries made little of it, that the Muslim army may only have been raiding, and that its fame grew mainly in the nineteenth century (Palmer 2019).
- **The year 1000.** Following Georges Duby, Thomas Bisson described a sudden "feudal revolution", in which castle lords seized the powers to judge, command and tax that had belonged to kings and counts (Bisson 1994); Dominique Barthélemy argues for slower change, made to look sudden by a shift in the surviving documents (Barthélemy 1997).

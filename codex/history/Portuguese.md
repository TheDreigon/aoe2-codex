# Portuguese

## Who they were

The codex's Portuguese represent the kingdom of Portugal from 1139, when Afonso Henriques took the royal title, to Manuel I's death in 1521, with a core from King Dinis (1279) to the first India fleets (1500). Two houses ruled: the Burgundian line of Afonso's father, Count Henry, until 1383, and Avis from 1385. From western Iberia it expanded after 1415 to Moroccan strongholds, Atlantic islands and West African trading posts, and after 1498 to the Indian Ocean. Its name comes from Portucale, the county around Porto; its language, Galician-Portuguese, was at first shared with Galicia.

## Rise and height

Afonso Henriques won León's acceptance of his title at Zamora (1143) and the pope's in 1179. He took Lisbon in 1147 with crusaders bound for the Holy Land, and Afonso III completed the conquest of the Algarve in 1249. Dinis (1279–1325) rebuilt many frontier castles, hired the Genoese Manuel Pessanha as admiral (1317) and had the pope found the Order of Christ, heir to the Templars' Portuguese estates, in 1319 (Oliveira Marques 1972). When Fernando I died in 1383, his heir was his daughter Beatriz, queen of Castile. Lisbon revolted, backing Fernando's half-brother João, head of the Order of Avis; the Cortes (the realm's assembly) made João king in April 1385, and on 14 August he routed a larger Castilian army at Aljubarrota (Lopes c. 1440s). João I and his sons took Ceuta in 1415; a ship sent by Prince Henry passed Cape Bojador, on the Saharan coast, in 1434. Under João II, the fort of São Jorge da Mina (1482), in today's Ghana, tapped the gold trade and Bartolomeu Dias rounded the Cape of Good Hope (1488); in 1498 Vasco da Gama reached India.

## War and society

Early kings fought Muslim al-Andalus with their households, religious orders of knights (Templars, Hospitallers, Santiago, Avis) and town militias. By the mid-fourteenth century each *concelho* (self-governing town) owed a quota (*conto*) of crossbowmen, mostly artisans who trained and mustered in return for tax exemptions; by the early fifteenth century these *besteiros do conto* numbered several thousand (Monteiro 1998). At Atoleiros (1384) the men of João's commander, Nuno Álvares Pereira, fought on foot against Castilian cavalry; at Aljubarrota the army held a chosen position behind ditches and pits, with dismounted men-at-arms in the centre and crossbowmen and English archers on the wings (Monteiro 2009). In Morocco, Portuguese light horsemen (*ginetes*) rode *à gineta* (short stirrups, light lance, javelins).

Salt pans at Setúbal, Aveiro and the Tagus estuary fed the trade in salted fish. After the Black Death (1348), Fernando I's Lei das Sesmarias (1375) ordered owners to farm their land or lose it. The Order of Christ helped fund Henry's voyages, which popes backed as a crusade (Russell 2000). Most African voyages used caravels, small ships with triangular (lateen) sails; to sail home from West Africa, pilots learned the *volta do mar*, a wide loop into the open Atlantic to catch westerly winds near the Azores (Diffie and Winius 1977). Columbus learned Atlantic sailing with the Portuguese before Castile sent him west in 1492. From 1441 ships brought back African captives, often seen as the start of the Atlantic slave trade; Zurara (1453) describes their division at Lagos, in the Algarve, in 1444. Trading posts (*feitorias*) exchanged horses, cloth and wheat for gold and enslaved Africans.

## End and legacy

By 1521 Pedro Álvares Cabral had reached Brazil (1500) and Afonso de Albuquerque had taken Goa (1510) and Malacca (1511); Portuguese power overseas rested on gun-armed naus (carracks), forts and trading posts. The Atlantic slave trade grew vastly in later centuries, above all to Brazil. The English alliance of 1373, renewed at Windsor in 1386, is still in force, and Portuguese is an official language on four continents.

## Contested

- **Prince Henry.** Older accounts made him the founder of a "school of Sagres" that planned the discoveries; Russell (2000) treats the school as a myth and Henry as a medieval crusading prince who also sought lordship and revenue. The epithet "Navigator" dates from the nineteenth century.
- **Aljubarrota.** Older accounts credit Nuno Álvares with a formation of his own, the "square"; Monteiro (2009) sees an able use of the English defensive style. Some think the Portuguese learned it from the English troops who campaigned in Portugal in 1381–1382; others see a Portuguese development, noting that Atoleiros (1384) came before the English archers of 1385.
- **Independence.** Nationalist historians saw a Portuguese nation seeking freedom from the start; Mattoso (1985) sees a struggle of princes and nobles, opened when Afonso Henriques defeated his mother's party at São Mamede (1128), and a national identity formed later. The tale of Christ appearing before the battle of Ourique (1139) is a later legend.

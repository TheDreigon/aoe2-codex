# Norwegians

## Who they were

The codex's Norwegians represent the kingdom of Norway and its North Atlantic realm, with the kingless Icelandic Commonwealth: together, the West Norse world. The civilization runs from the raid on Lindisfarne's monastery (793), the conventional start of the Viking Age, to 1469. The realm took in the Faroes, Orkney and Shetland (until 1468–69), the Hebrides and the Isle of Man (until 1266), Greenland (from 1261) and Iceland, a commonwealth from 930 and Norwegian from 1262–64. The name Norway, probably "the north way", is recorded around 890. The game's "Vikings" names an activity, not a people: in Old Norse, a *víkingr* (a word of debated origin) was a sea raider.

## Rise and height

Lindisfarne's raiders may have come from western Norway, since some versions of the *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle* say England's first recorded raiders, a few years earlier, came from Hordaland. In the ninth century the Norse settled Orkney, Shetland, the Faroes, the Hebrides and, from about 870, Iceland. Around 985 Eric the Red led settlers from Iceland to Greenland. Wood cut with European metal tools puts the Norse at L'Anse aux Meadows, in Newfoundland, in 1021 (Kuitems et al. 2022).

At home, the sagas credit Harald Fairhair's sea victory at Hafrsfjord, traditionally dated 872, with uniting Norway. Olaf Tryggvason (995–1000) and Olaf Haraldsson (1015–1028) imposed Christianity, often by force; Olaf Haraldsson, driven out in 1028, fell at Stiklestad in 1030 and was soon revered as St Olaf. Harald Hardrada died invading England at Stamford Bridge in 1066. In 1152/53 Nidaros (Trondheim) became an archbishopric whose authority reached from Greenland to Man. Haakon IV (1217–1263) ended a century of civil wars, and Greenland and Iceland accepted Norwegian rule. His son Magnus the Law-mender ceded the Hebrides and Man to Scotland (1266) and issued a law for the whole kingdom (1274).

## War and society

Norwegian armies fought mainly on foot, in a shield wall; Viking-age mail was costly and rare. A poem of around 900, *Haraldskvæði*, puts berserkir, warriors in a battle fury, among Harald Fairhair's men. The Icelandic historian Snorri (c. 1230) pictures Odin's legendary berserkir fighting without mail and biting their shields. Norway's strength was the ship, clinker-built of overlapping planks, as the ships buried at Oseberg (834) and Gokstad (c. 900) show. Later laws describe the levy (*leiðangr*): each ship district had to build, man and supply a warship when the king called. The law of western Norway's Gulaþing assembly required a bow and 24 arrows for every rowing bench (Larson 1935), and sea fights opened with archery before boarding. By the thirteenth century the levy was commonly paid as a tax.

The Atlantic islands lived on the settlers' sheep and cattle: the Faroes' name probably means "sheep islands", and Iceland used homespun wool cloth (*vaðmál*) as money. Norway's great export was stockfish, cod dried on racks in the north. Some cod eaten at Haithabu, a Viking-age Danish town, came from Arctic waters off northern Norway (Star et al. 2017), and Bergen later shipped stockfish across Europe. Kings built castles from the twelfth century, and St Olaf's shrine at Nidaros drew pilgrims.

## End and legacy

The Black Death (1349) killed half or more of Norway's people. Norway shared its kings with Sweden in 1319–55 and with Denmark from 1380. Merchants of the Hanse, a league of German towns, dominated Bergen's stockfish trade. Norse Greenland's last written record is a wedding at Hvalsey church in 1408; its settlers died out or left later that century. In 1468–69 Christian I pledged Orkney and Shetland to Scotland as security for his daughter's dowry, a pledge never redeemed. The Gaelic lords' galley, probably descended from the Norse ship, sailed the Hebrides into the seventeenth century (Rixson 1998).

## In the codex

The shield wall stands behind *Infantry +20% HP starting in Feudal Age* and the shipwrights behind *Warships cost -10/15/20% in Feudal/Castle/Imperial Age*; stockfish gives *Fishing Ships generate gold in addition to food*, and herds and settlers give *Shepherds work +25% faster* and *New Town Centers spawn 2 Sheep starting in Castle Age*. Shipbuilding passed to the Gaels gives the team bonus, *Docks cost -15%*. The *Berserk* renders Harald Fairhair's berserkir, *Leiðangr* the levy and *Bogsveigar* ("bow-benders") its archers; castles and the Church add the Keep and three Monastery techs.

## Contested

- **Harald Fairhair.** The sagas made him the unifier of all Norway. Many historians now date Hafrsfjord to the 880s, not 872, and place the core of his kingdom in south-western Norway; most agree that unification gained real momentum only in the eleventh century (Krag 2003).
- **The levy's age.** Snorri (c. 1230) credits Haakon the Good (mid-tenth century) with the ship districts, but the laws that describe them date from the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. Some scholars read eleventh-century court poetry as evidence of royal levy fleets; others argue that in neighbouring Denmark, whose levy was similar, a public levy took shape only in the twelfth century.
- **Berserkir.** Most accounts of them were written down centuries after Hafrsfjord, often casting them as villains, and Icelandic law made going berserk an offence. Historians debate whether they were a real cult of Odin's warriors, perhaps fighting in a ritual trance, or largely a literary type; theories that they took mushrooms or henbane are speculative.

# Aztecs

## Who they were

The Aztecs here are the Mexica, or Tenochca, of Tenochtitlan, and the Triple Alliance (Excan Tlahtoloyan) they formed with Texcoco and Tlacopan. Nahuatl speakers like most of their neighbours, they lived in the Basin of Mexico, a high valley of lakes and city-states (altepetl), and came to dominate much of central Mesoamerica. ‘Aztec’, from Aztlan, the homeland of their migration legends, is a later scholarly name (Smith 2012). The civilization runs from the traditional foundation of Tenochtitlan (1325) to its fall (1521), with its core in 1428–1500.

## Rise and height

The Mexica reached the Basin late, after a migration whose historicity is debated. They founded Tenochtitlan on an island in Lake Texcoco and until 1428 served the Tepanecs of Azcapotzalco as tributaries and mercenaries. That year their ruler Itzcoatl, allied with Nezahualcoyotl of Texcoco and with Tlacopan, overthrew Azcapotzalco. Under Moctezuma I (1440–1469) the alliance's armies reached the Gulf coast and Oaxaca. Axayacatl (1469–1481) annexed the twin city of Tlatelolco in 1473 but was routed by the Purépecha (Tarascans) in the late 1470s. Ahuitzotl (1486–1502) rededicated the Templo Mayor in 1487, and his armies reached Xoconochco (Soconusco) on the Pacific. At its height the alliance drew tribute from the 38 provinces recorded in the Codex Mendoza (Berdan et al. 1996). Tlaxcala and the Purépecha stayed independent, but the empire was still expanding under Moctezuma II (1502–1520) when the Spaniards came.

## War and society

All Mexica boys received military training, commoners in their ward's school (telpochcalli) and nobles in the temple school (calmecac), so armies could be raised quickly. Battles opened with arrows and sling stones, then elite warriors threw atlatl darts as the lines closed for hand-to-hand fighting with obsidian-edged clubs (macuahuitl). Warriors rose in rank by taking captives; the eagle and jaguar orders, whose members were often nobles, ranked below only the otontin and cuachicqueh (Hassig 1988). Without siege engines, wars were mostly decided in the field; burning a town's main temple, the Codex Mendoza's sign of conquest, sealed its defeat. Conquered towns usually kept their rulers but owed tribute, gathered by stewards (calpixqueh): food, cotton mantles, cacao, feathers and gold (Berdan et al. 1996). Against Tlaxcala, Huexotzinco and Cholula the alliance fought recurrent ‘flower wars’ (xochiyaoyotl), which the sources present as prearranged.

Chinampas, raised fields of lake mud in the southern lakes of Xochimilco and Chalco (Smith 2012), helped feed a capital of perhaps 150,000 to 250,000 people. With neither draft animals nor wheeled transport, goods moved on porters' backs or by canoe. Cacao beans and cotton mantles served as money; Cortés reckoned the Tlatelolco market drew over sixty thousand people a day (Cortés 1520), and the pochteca, guilds of long-distance merchants, also spied for the state (Sahagún c. 1577). Huitzilopochtli, the Mexica patron god, and Tlaloc, the rain god, shared the Templo Mayor; many feasts culminated in the sacrifice of war captives, usually by removal of the heart.

## End and legacy

Hernán Cortés landed in 1519, won Tlaxcala as an ally and entered Tenochtitlan in November. After the Spaniards massacred celebrants at the Toxcatl feast in May 1520, the city revolted; Moctezuma, by then a Spanish captive, died in disputed circumstances, and the Spaniards fled. Smallpox, brought by Pánfilo de Narváez's expedition in April 1520, then killed many, including the new ruler, Cuitlahuac. In 1521 Cortés besieged the city with thirteen brigantines and tens of thousands of indigenous allies, above all Tlaxcalans, and on 13 August the last independent ruler, Cuauhtemoc, was captured (Townsend 2019). Mexico City rose on the ruins. Nahuatl still had 1.65 million speakers in 2020, and Mexico's name and the eagle on its flag come from the Mexica and their founding legend.

## Contested

- **The flower wars.** Durán (1581) credits the royal counsellor Tlacaelel, under Moctezuma I, with arranging the recurrent wars with Tlaxcala and its neighbours as a nearby source of captives for sacrifice; Hassig (1988) reads them as a low-cost way to wear down, contain and overawe enemies too strong to conquer at once.
- **The scale of sacrifice.** Durán (1581) reports 80,400 victims at the rededication of the Templo Mayor in 1487, a figure many modern scholars consider greatly inflated; the great skull rack (Huey Tzompantli), excavated by archaeologists of Mexico's INAH since 2015, confirms that sacrifice took place on a large scale without fixing its numbers.
- **Moctezuma and Quetzalcoatl.** The story that Moctezuma took Cortés for the returning god Quetzalcoatl, told in Sahagún's Florentine Codex and long repeated as fact, first appears in accounts written decades after the conquest; Townsend (2019) and others argue that it was a post-conquest construction.

# Lithuanians

## Who they were

The codex's Lithuanians represent the Grand Duchy of Lithuania from the victory of Saule (1236) to 1500. A Baltic people whose land is first named in 1009, they lived on the Neris and Neman rivers, with Samogitia (the "lowland") to the west. Their grand dukes came to rule the Ruthenian lands of the old Rus' on the upper Dnieper, whose Orthodox Slavs were most of their subjects by about 1350; under Vytautas the realm reached the Black Sea. From 1385 it was in union with Poland. The Lithuanian heartland itself stayed pagan until its formal baptism in 1387, Samogitia until 1413–17 (Baronas and Rowell 2015).

## Rise and height

At Saule, on 22 September 1236, Samogitians and Semigallians crushed the Sword Brothers, Livonia's crusading order, whose survivors joined the Teutonic Order as its Livonian branch. Mindaugas, the leading duke by the 1240s, split a coalition of rivals by winning over one of its allies, the Livonian Order: he accepted Latin (Catholic) baptism about 1251 and in 1253 became Lithuania's only crowned king, on Pope Innocent IV's authority. He broke with the Order in 1261; rivals murdered him in 1263. Gediminas (1316–1341) made Vilnius his capital; his letters of 1323, the earliest mention of Vilnius, invited merchants, craftsmen and friars and offered to accept baptism, a plan he dropped in 1324 (Rowell 1994). His son Algirdas (1345–1377) beat the Tatars at Blue Waters (1362 or 1363) and took Kiev, while his brother Kęstutis held the west. Algirdas's son Jogaila considered a Muscovite bride and Orthodox baptism but chose Poland: at Krewo (1385) he promised Latin baptism for himself and his people, then married Queen Jadwiga and became king of Poland (1386). His cousin Vytautas, ruler of Lithuania from 1392, crushed the Teutonic Order's army with Jogaila at Grunwald on 15 July 1410.

## War and society

The Order's edge lay in big warhorses and crossbows; Lithuanians at first rode small native horses, often to fight on foot, and from about 1300 bred larger ones (Ekdahl 1998). Both sides raided across the wilderness of forest and marsh between Prussia and Lithuania: the Order's chronicler Peter of Dusburg tells of Lithuanian raids for captives and plunder, and the Order's crusading *Reisen* ("journeys") drew knights from across Latin Europe (Christiansen 1997). Many of Lithuania's hundreds of hill forts (*piliakalniai*) became timber castles on the Neman and the Neris. From the 14th century, heavy cavalry came from the boyars, Lithuanian and Ruthenian landowners who served with their retinues; at the union of Horodło (1413) Catholic boyars gained the Polish nobles' privileges (Kiaupa et al. 2000). After his steppe campaigns of 1397–98 Vytautas settled Tatars near Vilnius and Trakai, and Tatar horse archers fought at Grunwald. In the Polish–Lithuanian campaigns of 1410–22, Lithuanian and Ruthenian light horsemen, with Tatars at least in 1410, ravaged the Order's lands.

Most people farmed and kept livestock, but the main exports were forest goods: wax above all, honey and furs. The old religion honoured gods such as Perkūnas, the thunder god, in sacred groves and at a sacred fire in Vilnius, and princes were cremated with their horses and arms, as Kęstutis was in 1382.

## End and legacy

Vytautas died in 1430 before he could be crowned king of Lithuania; after a civil war, Jogaila's son Casimir ruled Lithuania from 1440 and Poland from 1447. Moscow, claiming all the Rus' lands, routed the Lithuanians at the Vedrosha in 1500. In the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth created by the Union of Lublin (1569), the grand duchy kept its own army, treasury and laws until the last partition (1795). Lithuanians, Belarusians and Ukrainians all claim its heritage.

## In the codex

The forest economy, the light war of the wilderness, the hill forts and pagan resistance stand behind *Foragers work +15% faster*, *Spearman-line and Skirmisher-line move +10% faster*, *Town Centers +3 range starting in Castle Age* and *Units more resistant to conversion*; raiding for the Polish ally gives the team bonus, *Scout Cavalry-line +2 attack vs. buildings*. The *Boyar*, *Horodło* and *Lipka Tatars* (the community's later name) render the boyars, their privileges and Vytautas's Tatars. The light horsemen become the *Sulica Rider* (a modern name), the regional Genitour named after the sulica, a Rus' and Polish throwing spear that Lithuanians may have used.

## Contested

- **Saule.** No account names the pagan commander. Since the later nineteenth century many historians have named the Samogitian duke Vykintas, whom the Galician-Volhynian Chronicle credits with killing many of the Order's knight-brothers; no source places Mindaugas there.
- **Mindaugas's faith.** Whether he returned to paganism after 1261 is debated: several 13th- and 14th-century sources say so, and the hostile Galician-Volhynian Chronicle calls even his baptism a sham, but in 1268 Pope Clement IV, elected after his death, still wrote of "Mindaugas of bright memory" (*clare memorie Mindota*).
- **The withdrawal at Grunwald.** After about an hour, much of the Lithuanian wing fell back and some of the Order's troops broke ranks to pursue. The Polish chronicler Jan Długosz describes a rout; many historians see a steppe-style feigned retreat, citing an undated, nearly contemporary German letter, published by Ekdahl (1963), that warned the Grand Master against such ruses.

# Poles

## Who they were

The codex's Poles represent the Polish duchy and kingdom under the Piast and Jagiellonian dynasties, from Mieszko I's baptism in 966 to 1500, with a focus on the kingdom reunited in 1320. Its heartlands were Greater Poland (Poznań, Gniezno) and Lesser Poland (Kraków), with Kuyavia, Sieradz and Łęczyca. Most Silesian dukes became Bohemian vassals from 1327; Red Ruthenia, around Lviv, was won in the 1340s and Royal Prussia, with Gdańsk, in 1454–66; Masovia's dukes were mostly crown vassals from 1351. The name is usually traced to the Polanie, "people of the fields".

## Rise and height

Mieszko I married the Bohemian princess Dobrawa in 965 and was baptized in 966; the chronicler Thietmar credits her influence, while historians add political aims: the Bohemian alliance and fewer pretexts for German attacks. His son Bolesław I the Brave won Poland its own archbishop at Gniezno (1000) and a crown (1025). After the state collapsed in the 1030s, Casimir I the Restorer rebuilt it from Kraków. From 1138 Bolesław III's heirs divided the duchy, and no king was crowned until 1295. In 1308 the Teutonic Order, called in against the pagan Baltic Prussians in the 1220s, seized Gdańsk Pomerania.

Władysław the Elbow-high was crowned at Kraków in 1320. His son Casimir III the Great (1333–70) gave up claims to Silesia and Pomerania, won Red Ruthenia and founded Kraków's university in 1364 (Knoll 1972). At Krewo in 1385 Jogaila (Jagiełło), pagan grand duke of Lithuania, promised baptism for the hand of young Queen Jadwiga, daughter of Casimir's successor Louis of Hungary; baptized as Władysław, he was crowned in 1386. Polish and Lithuanian armies crushed the Order's army at Grunwald (1410), though the peace of 1411 gained little land. Under Jogaila's son Casimir IV, the Thirteen Years' War (1454–66) won Royal Prussia and made the Order a Polish vassal for its remaining lands.

## War and society

Tree-ring dates show that Mieszko's forebears built earth-and-timber strongholds (*grody*) at Poznań and Giecz in the 920s and at Gniezno around 940, and that he enlarged them (Urbańczyk 2012); the chronicler Jan Długosz credits Casimir III with dozens of castles and town walls. Knights fought in banners (*chorągwie*) of "lances" (*kopie*), each an armoured knight, the *kopijnik*, with mounted retainers; Długosz counts fifty Polish banners at Grunwald. Registers of the king's court banner from 1495–1501 show a minority of fully armoured riders among lighter-armoured ones, many with crossbows, and some light horse armed in the Rascian (Serbian) or Tatar manner (Kałużny 2023).

Nobles served at their own cost within the kingdom in the general levy (*pospolite ruszenie*), and kings bought their support with privileges (Davies 1981): Košice (1374) freed noble land from almost all taxes, and the Nieszawa statute for Greater Poland (1454) barred calling the levy without its local noble assemblies' consent. After the Order routed the levy at Chojnice (1454), the crown hired companies of horse and foot, which won at Świecino (1462). Hired foot fought mainly with crossbows until hand-held guns spread in the 1490s (Grabarczyk 2021).

Salt from the royal mines of Wieliczka and Bochnia, near Kraków, is traditionally reckoned at about a third of Casimir III's revenue. In the fifteenth century lords expanded their own farms (*folwarki*), worked by peasants' compulsory labour, and shipped grain down the Vistula to Gdańsk; the statute of Piotrków (1496) curbed peasants' freedom to leave. Refounded in 1400 with Jadwiga's bequest, Kraków's university drew students from across Central Europe, Copernicus among them (1491–95).

## End and legacy

Around 1500 Casimir IV's sons ruled Poland, Lithuania, Bohemia (from 1471) and Hungary (from 1490). By 1493 the Sejm (parliament) had two chambers, a senate and the nobles' envoys. The Union of Lublin (1569) made Poland and Lithuania one Commonwealth until the partitions of 1772–95.

## Contested

- **The Black Death.** No contemporary source mentions Casimir III's supposed plague quarantine. Pollen records show Polish farming still growing through the fourteenth century, suggesting a lighter toll than in France or Scandinavia (Izdebski et al. 2022); written sources are too few to measure it.
- **Jadwiga and Krewo.** Jadwiga was eleven when the lords of Lesser Poland, her mother's envoys and Jogaila's envoys agreed the match; how far she shaped it is debated. In 1387 she led the recovery of Red Ruthenia from Hungary. Jogaila also promised to "join" (*applicare*) his lands to the Polish crown; many Polish historians read this as incorporation or vassalage, Lithuanian historians as a union under one ruler.
- **The Polanie.** Urbańczyk (2012) finds no first-millennium source for a Polan tribe and suggests the Piasts came from Great Moravia; most historians favour a local origin in Greater Poland.

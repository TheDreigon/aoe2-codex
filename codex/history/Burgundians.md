# Burgundians

## Who they were

The codex's Burgundians are the Valois dukes of Burgundy and their lands, from 1363 to 1477. In 1363 King John II of France granted the duchy to his youngest son, Philip the Bold; in 1477 Charles the Bold died at Nancy. Besides the duchy and the county of Burgundy (Franche-Comté), the dukes gathered Flanders, Artois, Brabant, Hainaut, Holland, Zeeland, Luxembourg and other lordships, some in France, others in the Holy Roman Empire. Historians call the whole the Burgundian state or, for the Low Countries, the Burgundian Netherlands. The name Burgundy recalls a Germanic people conquered by the Franks in 534.

## Rise and height

In 1369 Philip married Margaret of Male, heiress of Flanders; in 1384 they inherited Flanders, Artois and the county of Burgundy (Vaughan 1962). Their son John the Fearless had the French king's brother, Louis of Orléans, murdered in 1407. In the civil war that followed, men of the king's heir killed John during a meeting at Montereau (1419). His son Philip the Good (1419–1467) allied with England until 1435, when he made peace with the French king at Arras. By purchase, inheritance, treaty and force he added Namur, Hainaut, Holland, Zeeland, Brabant and Luxembourg (Vaughan 1970). At the Bruges feasts for his marriage to Isabella of Portugal (1430) he founded the Order of the Golden Fleece, a brotherhood of knights sworn to him. Their son Charles the Bold (1467–1477) created a standing army in 1471 and in 1473 sought a royal crown from Emperor Frederick III, in vain. After failing to take Neuss (1474–75), he seized Lorraine, the gap between his northern and southern lands.

## War and society

Before 1471 ducal armies mixed noble retinues, town contingents and hired troops, and archers usually outnumbered men-at-arms (armoured horsemen). For 1465 the chronicler Commynes reckoned some 1,400 men-at-arms and several thousand archers. Charles's decrees of 1471–73 grouped his standing army in "lances". Each had a man-at-arms, his page, his coustillier (an attendant named after his coustille, a long knife) and three archers who rode to war but usually fought on foot. Crossbowmen, handgunners and pikemen served beside them (Vaughan 1973). Towns had sworn guilds of crossbowmen (Saint George) and archers (Saint Sebastian) that trained and held contests (Crombie 2016). The ducal artillery was among Europe's largest, from great bombards such as Mons Meg (1449) to handguns (Smith and DeVries 2005).

The Low Countries, the most urbanised region north of the Alps, supplied most ducal revenue from cloth and trade (Blockmans and Prevenier 1999). Bruges hosted German, Italian and Iberian merchants and bankers. In 1395 Philip the Bold banned the Gamay grape to protect the quality of Burgundy wine. In 1433–34 Philip the Good gave Flanders, Brabant, Hainaut and Holland a common coinage with silver *vierlanders*, "four-lands" coins (Spufford 1970). Town militias, famed for beating French knights at Courtrai (1302) with pikes and goedendags (spiked clubs), served the dukes but often fought them; Ghent's militia was crushed at Gavere (1453). Nobles jousted in staged challenges (*pas d'armes*), and at the Feast of the Pheasant (Lille, 1454) the duke and his knights vowed a crusade against the Ottomans, never fulfilled.

## End and legacy

In 1476 the Swiss routed Charles at Grandson, taking much of his artillery, and again at Morat. On 5 January 1477 he fell at Nancy, fighting René II of Lorraine, whom he had ousted, and René's Swiss mercenaries. Louis XI of France took the duchy, but Charles's only child, Mary, married Maximilian of Habsburg and kept most of the Low Countries, which passed to their grandson Charles V. These lands became the core of Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg, and the Order of the Golden Fleece still exists.

## In the codex

Low Countries wealth gives *Economic upgrades available one age earlier and cost -40% food*, and Bruges' trade gives the team bonus *Trade Units generate +15% gold*. Archers, jousting nobles and ducal guns give *Archer-line costs -10/20% in Castle/Imperial Age*, *Cavalier upgrade available in Castle Age* and *Gunpowder Units +25% attack*. The *Coustillier* comes from Charles's lances, the *Flemish Militia* from the armed towns and the *Arbalester*, added to the tech tree, from the crossbow guilds. The unique techs are *Vierlander Coinage*, *Burgundian Vineyards* and the *Order of the Golden Fleece*.

## Contested

- **A state or a union of lands?** Vaughan (1962) and Schnerb (1999) describe a Burgundian state taking shape. The term is debated, since each land kept its own laws and assemblies and the dukes never won a royal crown. Blockmans and Prevenier (1999) stress the dukes' lasting work in drawing the Low Countries together.
- **Joan of Arc.** Men of John of Luxembourg, a Burgundian commander, captured her at Compiègne in 1430, and John sold his captive to the English. Philip the Good, England's ally, was at the siege and visited the prisoner, but how far he shaped the sale is unclear.
- **The court.** Huizinga (1919) saw the court's chivalry and feasts as the "autumn" of a fading age; more recent historians treat them as deliberate tools of princely power.

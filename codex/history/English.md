# English

## Who they were

The codex's English are the kingdom of England from the Norman Conquest (1066) to the battle of Bosworth (1485), with a design core from Edward I's Welsh wars (1277) to the defeat at Castillon (1453). England's kings also held a lordship in Ireland from 1171, all of Wales from 1283 and lands in France: Gascony from 1154, Calais from 1347 and, again in 1417–50, Normandy. The game calls them "Britons", but in English scholarship that name means the island's earlier, Celtic-speaking people and their heirs, the Welsh, Cornish and Bretons. After 1066 the court and nobility spoke French, yet within a few generations their descendants called themselves English. England before 1066 belongs to the codex's Anglo-Saxons.

## Rise and height

William of Normandy won at Hastings in 1066 and crushed English revolts by 1071. Henry II (1154–1189) also ruled Normandy, Anjou and Aquitaine; his son John lost Normandy in 1204 and had to grant Magna Carta in 1215. Edward I (1272–1307) conquered Wales by 1283, but his Scottish war led to his son's defeat at Bannockburn (1314). In 1337 Philip VI of France confiscated Gascony, starting the Hundred Years War, and Edward III (1327–1377) claimed the French crown. His fleet won at Sluys (1340) and his army at Crécy (1346), and Calais fell in 1347. In 1356 his son Edward, later called the Black Prince, captured John II of France at Poitiers. The French recovered most of their losses in the 1370s. Henry V (1413–1422) took Harfleur, won at Agincourt (1415) and conquered Normandy, and the Treaty of Troyes (1420) made him heir to the French throne.

## War and society

Wool, England's chief export, funded the wars through customs from 1275 and taxes voted by Parliament; from 1363, with interruptions, most wool exports went through the Staple at Calais, a compulsory market (Lloyd 1977). Kings and lords founded new towns, like the boroughs beside Edward I's castles in Wales. Many villages rotated crops across two or three unfenced "open fields", one resting each year.

From 1181 the Crown required free men to keep arms suited to their wealth, from the 1240s including bows for poorer men; in 1363 Edward III ordered archery practice on holidays. From Dupplin Moor (1332) and Halidon Hill (1333), men-at-arms (fully armoured soldiers, including knights) fought on foot in the centre, flanked by massed archers: the formation of Crécy and Agincourt. Whether the longbow outranged the crossbow is debated, but its faster shooting is not: an archer shot several arrows while a crossbowman reloaded once (Strickland and Hardy 2005). The *Gesta Henrici Quinti*, by an unnamed royal chaplain, reports that before Agincourt Henry V had each archer cut a stake to plant against charging horses. Mounted archers and men-at-arms swept across France in Edward III's chevauchées, great raids that burned and plundered (Rogers 2000). Guns were tried against Calais and battered Harfleur, and Henry V's navy added captured Genoese carracks. English archers also served allies and employers, from Pedro I of Castile at Nájera (1367) to Italian cities under John Hawkwood.

## End and legacy

After Joan of Arc lifted the siege of Orléans (1429) and England's ally Burgundy made peace with Charles VII (1435), the French retook Normandy (1449–50) and Gascony, where their guns broke the English attack at Castillon (1453). On the Continent, only Calais stayed English, until 1558. From 1455 the houses of Lancaster and York fought the Wars of the Roses, which Henry Tudor's victory over Richard III at Bosworth (1485) effectively ended. Medieval England passed on the common law, Parliament and the language of Chaucer.

## In the codex

Wool and new towns stand behind *Shepherds work +25% faster* and *Town Centers cost -50% wood starting in Castle Age*; the longbow behind the *Longbowman* and *Foot Archers attack +10/+20% faster in Castle/Imperial Age*; dismounted men-at-arms behind *Militia-line +1/+2 melee armor in Castle/Imperial Age*; and archers who served allies behind the team bonus, *Archery Ranges work +10% faster*. The arms laws become *Yeomen*, and the raids the *Chevauchée*, replacing the Warwolf, a trebuchet built for one siege. Guns, carracks and open fields add the Bombard Cannon, the Carrack and Crop Rotation; the Mounted Crossbowman leaves, since English mounted archers shot longbows on foot.

## Contested

- **Agincourt's numbers.** Working from administrative records, Curry (2005) counts about 9,000 English against about 12,000 French; Barker (2005), closer to the chroniclers, sees about 6,000 English outnumbered at least four to one.
- **The longbow's effect.** DeVries (1997) suggests that the arrows worked mainly on the enemy's morale rather than by killing many; Rogers (1998) defends the long-held view that the archers' power to strike down charging men-at-arms was decisive from Halidon Hill (1333) to Verneuil (1424). Modern tests also disagree on how well arrows pierced plate armour.
- **What the chevauchées were for.** Rogers (2000) argues that Edward III raided to force the French to fight on ground of his choosing and to ruin the French king's revenues; others see the raids mainly as plunder and economic war that avoided battle.

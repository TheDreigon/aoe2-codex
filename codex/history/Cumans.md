# Cumans

## Who they were

The Cumans were Turkic-speaking nomadic herders who displaced or absorbed earlier nomads, the Oghuz and Pechenegs, and held the steppe from the Danube to the Irtysh river. Western Europeans and Byzantines called them Cumans, the Rus' Polovtsy, the Hungarians Kun, and Muslim and Turkic writers Qipchaq (Kipchak), hence Desht-i Qipchaq, the Persian name for their steppe. The Cuman-Kipchak confederation had no single ruler: each horde, a group of clans with its own camps, followed its own khan (chief). The codex centres on its western, Cuman branch from 1055 to 1241, then follows its diaspora to about 1400.

## Rise and height

The Cumans first met the Rus' in 1055, raided them from 1061 and in 1068 routed three sons of Yaroslav the Wise on the Alta river. In 1091 Cuman khans, among them Tugorkan, helped the Byzantines wipe out the Pechenegs at Levounion. In 1096 Khan Boniak reached Kiev and sacked the Monastery of the Caves. Pressed by Rus' campaigns in 1103–1111, Khan Atrak took his people to Georgia, where his son-in-law, King David IV, settled them as soldiers who helped him win at Didgori (1121).

In 1185 Atrak's son Konchak and other khans routed the army of Prince Igor of Novgorod-Seversky on the Kayala river and captured him. Driven across the Danube by Byzantium in 1186, the rebel brothers Peter and Asen returned with Cuman allies and restored an independent Bulgaria. With Cuman horse archers, their brother Kaloyan captured Baldwin I, the crusaders' emperor of Constantinople, at Adrianople (1205). In 1223 Mongol raiders routed the Cumans and their Rus' allies on the Kalka river.

## War and society

According to the crusader Robert de Clari, every Cuman had ten or twelve horses following him, and they rode six to eight days' journey in a day and a night. Most fought as horse archers, with sabres and lances too; richer men wore mail or lamellar (armour of laced plates). Ambush and feigned flight were their hallmark: at Adrianople they drew the crusader knights into a reckless pursuit. They seem to have besieged towns rarely, mostly by blockade; Konchak's huge crossbows and his Muslim specialist who shot "living fire" (1184–1185) are an isolated record.

Herds were their wealth. Clari says they neither ploughed nor sowed, living in felt tents on milk, cheese and meat. Raids brought plunder and captives for ransom or sale. The historian Ibn al-Athir called the Crimean port of Sudak the Kipchaks' city, where cloth was traded for slaves and furs.

Khans met in council: in 1103, facing a Rus' invasion, Urusoba urged peace, but the younger men overruled him. Khans' daughters married into the ruling houses of Rus', Georgia, Bulgaria and Hungary; Sviatopolk II of Kiev wed Tugorkan's daughter in 1094. The Cumans revered Tengri, the sky god. On burial mounds (kurgans) they set stone statues of the dead, each holding a cup, as the friar William of Rubruck saw in 1253.

## End and legacy

Mongol armies under Batu broke the confederation in 1237–1241. Khan Köten took his people to Hungary in 1239; when the Mongols invaded in March 1241, Hungarians in Pest, suspecting treason, killed him, and his Cumans ravaged their way into Bulgaria. Those left on the steppe became most of the Golden Horde's nomads, and its Mongol rulers in time took up their Kipchak Turkic speech. In Egypt, slave soldiers (mamluks), largely Kipchaks, took the throne in 1250, and until 1382 most sultans, such as Baybars, were Kipchaks or their descendants. In Bulgaria, George Terter, of Cuman descent, became tsar in 1280 (Vásáry 2005). Recalled to Hungary around 1246, the Cumans slowly became Catholic Hungarians (Berend 2001); the Kunság region keeps their name.

## Contested

- **Origins and identity.** Around 1120 al-Marwazi of Merv told of a people called Qun who fled the Khitans of northern China, pushing other nomads west. Some scholars identify the Qun with the Cumans; others trace Cuman origins to the Kimek confederation on the Irtysh river, which included the Kipchaks. Whether Cumans and Kipchaks were one people, two branches of one confederation or two associated peoples is also debated (Golden 1992).
- **Herders or farmers.** Clari (c. 1216) says the Cumans neither ploughed nor sowed; the archaeologist Pletneva (1990) sees twelfth-century hordes with fixed seasonal pastures and the first signs of settlement and farming.
- **Elizabeth the Cuman.** Many historians think Elizabeth, wife of Stephen V of Hungary, was Köten's daughter. Others name Seyhan, a chief whom Stephen's father, King Béla IV, called his kinsman; "Seyhan" may be a title, so the two may be one man.

# Berbers

## Who they were

The codex's Berbers are the Amazigh peoples of the Maghreb (Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia and western Libya; Arabic for "the West") and the western Sahara, above all the Sanhaja, Masmuda and Zenata confederations, and the states they founded. Their window runs from Kusaila's victory over the Arab general Uqba ibn Nafi at Tahudha (683) to the Wattasids' fall (1554), centred on the Almoravids and Almohads (1040–1269), who also ruled al-Andalus (Muslim Iberia). "Berber" comes from Arabic *Barbar*, probably from Greek and Latin words for "barbarians"; many Berbers now call themselves Imazighen (singular Amazigh).

## Rise and height

The Arab conquest (c. 647–709) met long resistance from Kusaila, then Dihya, called al-Kahina ("the seer"). Tariq ibn Ziyad, a Berber in most sources, led an army, probably mostly Berber, into Iberia in 711. In 740 a revolt led by Kharijites (egalitarian Muslim dissidents) ended caliphal rule in the western Maghreb. Kutama Berbers brought the Shi'i Fatimids to power (909) and to Egypt (969); the Sanhaja Zirids ruled Ifriqiya (roughly Tunisia) for them from 972, breaking away around 1048.

Around 1040 the jurist Ibn Yasin began preaching strict Sunni Islam of the Maliki school to the Saharan Sanhaja. His Almoravids (*al-murabitun*, from *ribat*, a fortified religious outpost) took the caravan towns of Sijilmasa and Awdaghust (1054–1055). Their leader Yusuf ibn Tashfin beat Alfonso VI of Castile at Zallaqa (Sagrajas) in 1086 and deposed most of the petty kings dividing al-Andalus (1090–1094). In 1121 Ibn Tumart, a Masmuda of the Atlas, proclaimed himself the Mahdi (the awaited redeemer); his successor Abd al-Mu'min took Marrakesh (1147) and Ifriqiya (1159–1160). The Almohads (*al-muwahhidun*, "affirmers of God's unity") routed Castile at Alarcos (1195) but lost at Las Navas de Tolosa (1212). Their empire split among the Hafsids of Tunis (1229), the Zayyanids of Tlemcen (1236) and the Zenata Marinids, who took Fez (1248) and Marrakesh (1269).

## War and society

The Andalusi geographer al-Bakri (1068) describes the early Almoravids fighting mostly on foot, long spears in the front rank and javelins behind, holding firm while their flag stood. He also mentions prized shields of *lamt* (antelope) hide. Later chroniclers say Yusuf's black African guard broke Alfonso VI's knights at Zallaqa. The most constant force was tribal light cavalry, above all the Zenata, on hardy Barb horses. Castile and Portugal adopted their short-stirrup riding, with javelins and leather shield; *jinete*, "light horseman", comes from *Zanati*, "of the Zenata". Rulers ringed cities with *tabiya*, earth and lime rammed into wooden frames: Yusuf's son Ali enclosed Marrakesh in 1126–1127; the Almohads walled Rabat and refortified Seville. Fleets ferried armies to Iberia, and in 1340 a Marinid fleet destroyed Castile's.

Sanhaja and Zenata nomads herded camels and sheep; Masmuda highlanders farmed Atlas valleys and moved flocks between seasonal pastures; villagers worked the Tell (the fertile coastal belt) and the oases (Brett and Fentress 1996). Caravans from Sijilmasa crossed the Sahara for West African gold, and Almoravid dinars were so trusted that Christian kings copied them as *morabitinos* (Messier 2010). Ibn Abi Zar (c. 1326) reports that in 1159–1160 Abd al-Mu'min had the Maghreb surveyed (*taksir*) for a land tax in grain and coin. The Almoravids enforced Maliki law; the Almohads opposed it and ended the protected status of Jews and Christians (Bennison 2016).

## End and legacy

The Marinid sultan Abu al-Hasan took Tlemcen (1337) and, briefly, Tunis (1347); in Iberia his defeat at the river Salado (1340) and loss of Algeciras (1344) ended the last great Maghrebi offensive. Portugal seized Ceuta (1415). In 1554 the Wattasids, Marinid kin ruling since 1472, fell to the Saadians, sharifs claiming descent from the Prophet; by 1574 the Ottomans had annexed the Zayyanid and Hafsid kingdoms. Almohad minarets survive in Marrakesh (Kutubiyya), Seville (Giralda) and Rabat (Hassan Tower). Tamazight (Berber) became official in Morocco (2011) and Algeria (2016).

## Contested

- **The name.** Rouighi (2019) argues that "Berber" did not exist as a category before the Arab conquest: the idea of one Berber people took shape in Arabic writing and was reworked by later authors. Brett and Fentress (1996), by contrast, trace the Berber-speaking peoples from prehistory to the present.
- **The Hilalian invasions.** Ibn Khaldun (late fourteenth century) blamed the Banu Hilal, Arab Bedouin who entered Ifriqiya around 1050, for ruining its countryside; Poncet (1967) called this "catastrophe" a myth built on fourteenth-century accusations, and the scale of the damage is still debated.
- **Zallaqa.** The decisive charge of Yusuf's black guard, and its size, come only from accounts written long after the battle; no contemporary account confirms them.

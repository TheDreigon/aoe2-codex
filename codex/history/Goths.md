# Goths

## Who they were

The codex's Goths are the two kingdoms that the Goths, an East Germanic people, ruled on Roman soil from 418 to 711. The Visigoths ruled from Toulouse, in Aquitaine, until 507, and later from Toledo. After 507 their kingdom lay in Hispania (Spain and Portugal) and Septimania, around Narbonne in southern France. The Ostrogoths ruled Italy from 493 until about 553, and Dalmatia until 536. Both usually called themselves "Goths"; the pairing of "West" and "East" Goths is a simplification by sixth-century writers (Wolfram 1988).

## Rise and height

Fleeing the Huns, Goths crossed the Danube into the Empire (376), killed the emperor Valens at Adrianople (378) and, under Alaric, sacked Rome (410). After fighting the Vandals and Alans in Hispania for Rome, King Wallia's Goths were settled in Aquitaine in 418 as federates (*foederati*), allies serving by treaty under their own kings. In 451 they fought beside the Roman general Aetius against Attila. King Euric (466–484) broke with Rome and expanded into Hispania. In 507 Clovis's Franks killed Alaric II at Vouillé and took most of Gothic Gaul.

Theodoric the Ostrogoth invaded Italy in 489 on the emperor Zeno's commission, took Ravenna in 493 and reigned until 526. After Vouillé his troops saved Septimania for the Visigoths, whom he ruled from 511 as regent for his young grandson Amalaric. In Hispania, King Leovigild (569–586) campaigned almost every year and annexed the north-western kingdom of the Sueves (585). He was the first Visigothic king to strike gold coins in his own name (Collins 2004) and, says Bishop Isidore of Seville, to sit on a throne in royal dress. In 654 King Recceswinth issued the *Liber Iudiciorum* ("Book of Judgements"), one law for Goths and Romans.

## War and society

The Goths' striking arm was the lance-armed horseman. Procopius, secretary to Belisarius, general of the eastern Roman emperor Justinian, says the Ostrogothic horsemen used only spear and sword; Belisarius credited his victories to mounted archers, which the Goths lacked. At Taginae (552) King Totila's cavalry charge broke under the arrows of 8,000 foot archers, by Procopius's count. Isidore says the Visigoths fought with lances and javelins and trusted most in their horsemen's swift charge. Levy laws added to the *Liber Iudiciorum* suggest armies were hard to raise: in 673 King Wamba obliged free men, clergy and magnates to answer the call, and in 681 King Ervig required them to bring a tenth of their slaves armed (Collins 2004).

The kings ruled from Roman cities over Roman estates, and Gothic settlers received shares of land or taxes. In Italy, Goths formed the army while Romans such as Cassiodorus ran the government. Theodoric built a palace, churches and a mausoleum at Ravenna and paid yearly for Rome's walls; Leovigild founded towns such as Reccopolis (578). Both peoples were Homoian ("Arian") Christians, who saw Christ as subordinate to God the Father; the Ostrogoths remained so, but the Visigoths turned Catholic under King Reccared at the Third Council of Toledo (589). The Gothic Bible, credited to the fourth-century bishop Wulfila, is best known from the silver-lettered Codex Argenteus, a Gospel book probably made for Theodoric.

## End and legacy

Justinian's war (535–554) destroyed the Ostrogothic kingdom. Totila fell at Taginae and the last king, Teia, near Vesuvius soon after; the remaining Goths probably merged gradually into Italy's population. In 711 a Berber and Arab army under Tariq ibn Ziyad defeated and probably killed King Roderic. Toledo soon fell, and Septimania held out into the 720s. By the 880s the Christian kings of Asturias, in northern Spain, claimed to be the Goths' heirs, and the *Liber Iudiciorum* lived on in Castilian as the *Fuero Juzgo* (thirteenth century).

## Contested

- **Old peoples or new ones?** Fourth-century sources name two main Gothic groups north of the lower Danube and the Black Sea, the Tervingi and the Greuthungi. Wolfram (1988) treats the Visigoths and Ostrogoths as these two peoples under other names; Heather (1996) sees both as new groups formed inside the Roman Empire after 376, as leaders such as Alaric and Theodoric united several Gothic bands.
- **The settlement of 418.** The Code of Euric speaks of Gothic "lots" (*sortes*) and Roman "thirds" (*tertiae*), long read as a division of estates between Goths and Roman landowners; Goffart (1980) argued that the Goths received shares of tax revenue instead, and the question is still debated.
- **Theodoric's status.** Arnold (2014) argues that his Roman subjects in Italy saw his kingdom as the Western Roman Empire restored and Theodoric as its emperor in all but name; Wiemer (2023) sees this as the "Roman" reading of Theodoric taken to extremes and notes that Constantinople never formally recognised him as a Western emperor.

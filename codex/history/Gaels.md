# Gaels

## Who they were

The codex's Gaels represent Gaelic Ireland and Scotland, with the medieval Scottish kingdom, from 563, when Columba founded the monastery of Iona, to 1500. They include the Irish kingdoms, Dál Riata in Argyll and Antrim, Alba (later Scotland) and the Lordship of the Isles, centred on the Hebrides, which Norway ceded to Scotland in 1266. Gaelic speakers called themselves *Goídil* (Gaels), and Latin writers called them *Scoti*. *Scotia*, their land, meant Ireland until the tenth or eleventh century, and then Alba. The game's "Celts" was an ancient name for continental peoples, first given to the Gaels by scholars after 1500. Scotland was never wholly Gaelic: English (later called Scots) spread from Lothian, and many lords had Anglo-Norman or Flemish roots.

## Rise and height

Ireland was a patchwork of small kingdoms (*túatha*) under provincial over-kings; the Uí Néill dynasties claimed the high kingship of Tara. Norse raiders set up a base at Dublin in 841. Brian Boru of Munster broke the Uí Néill hold on the high kingship in 1002. In 1014 he died at Clontarf, defeating Leinster, Norse Dublin and overseas allies such as the earl of Orkney (Duffy 2013). In northern Britain the Picts long overshadowed Dál Riata, yet by 900 the Pictish kingdom bore a Gaelic name, Alba (Woolf 2007). Anglo-Norman lords conquered much of Ireland after 1169; Gaelic victories at Callann (1261) and Áth an Chip (1270) checked them in Desmond and Connacht and began a long recovery. After Edward I of England invaded in 1296 and deposed King John Balliol, William Wallace and Andrew Murray beat his army at Stirling Bridge (1297). Robert Bruce, crowned in 1306, won at Bannockburn (1314); England renounced its claims in the Treaty of Edinburgh–Northampton (1328).

## War and society

Wealth was counted in cows, kept above all for milk: milk, butter and curds were everyday foods (Kelly 1997). War often meant the cattle raid (*creach*), the theme of the epic *Táin Bó Cúailnge*, "The Cattle Raid of Cooley". Monasteries produced manuscripts like the Book of Kells (c. 800). Monks went abroad as missionaries and exiles for Christ, as Columba had (Adomnán c. 700). Aidan of Iona founded Lindisfarne (635) and completed the conversion of Northumbria; Columbanus founded Luxeuil in Burgundy (c. 590) and Bobbio in Italy (614).

Gaelic armies were mostly light infantry. Gerald of Wales (c. 1188) describes the Irish fighting without armour, with short spears, javelins and heavy axes, and riding without saddles or spurs. Later sources call such soldiers kern (Irish *ceithern*, a war band). Irish lords hired galloglass (*gallóglaigh*, "foreign warriors"), heavy infantry from the Isles and Argyll in mail with long axes. The first recorded band, 160 men, came to Connacht with a Hebridean bride in 1259. By the fifteenth century, lords billeted galloglass on their subjects, who owed them food and lodging: the *buannacht* (Simms 1987). Short of siege engines, Gaels and Scots took castles by surprise or by climbing the walls, then demolished them. Bruce's men did so at Perth, Linlithgow, Roxburgh and Edinburgh in 1313–14 (Barbour 1375). Scottish spearmen fought in schiltrons, dense spear formations: Edward I's archers broke them at Falkirk (1298), but at Bannockburn they attacked and won. Fifteenth-century Scottish kings built up artillery: James II died when a gun burst at the siege of English-held Roxburgh (1460).

## End and legacy

In 1493 James IV seized the Lordship of the Isles, and by 1500 Scottish Gaelic was spoken mainly in the Highlands and Islands. In Ireland, Gaelic and Gaelicised Anglo-Irish lords ruled most of the island; the English Crown directly governed little more than Dublin and its hinterland. Gaelic Ireland lasted until the Tudor conquest, completed in 1603. Irish and Scottish Gaelic are still spoken.

## Contested

- **The Scots' origins.** Medieval tradition brought Dál Riata's kings from Antrim to Argyll around 500; Campbell (2001) finds no archaeological trace of a migration and sees a native Gaelic-speaking people, linked to Antrim by the sea.
- **From Pictland to Alba.** Tradition made Cináed mac Ailpín (Kenneth MacAlpin, d. 858) the first king of Scots, who conquered the Picts; Woolf (2007) notes that the annals call him king of the Picts and sees the Pictish kingdom itself becoming Gaelic, with "king of Alba" appearing only under his grandsons.
- **Clontarf.** Long told as the Irish victory that broke Viking power, it was recast by many later historians as the last act of a Leinster revolt against Brian, backed by Norse Dublin; Duffy (2013) argues that the many fighters from overseas made it much more than a rebellion.

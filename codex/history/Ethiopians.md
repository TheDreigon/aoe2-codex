# Ethiopians

## Who they were

The codex's Ethiopians are the Christian kingdom of the Ethiopian and Eritrean highlands from 500 to 1543. It spans late Aksum, a Red Sea power until about 630; the post-Aksumite kingdom and the Zagwe, to 1270; and its core, the Solomonic dynasty from 1270 to 1500. The heartland ran from Tigray southward through Lasta and Amhara to Shewa, with the port of Adulis in Aksumite times. King Ezana's fourth-century inscription names the highlanders "Ethiopians" in Greek and *Habashat* in Ge'ez, Aksum's language, which later borrowed the Greek name as *Ityopya*. Arabic *al-Habasha* gave "Abyssinia"; late medieval Europeans took its king for the legendary priest-king Prester John.

## Rise and height

The capital, Aksum, lay inland; its kings, Christian since Ezana's conversion in the mid-fourth century, minted gold coins (Munro-Hay 1991). Around 525 King Kaleb sailed against Himyar, in Yemen, and killed its Jewish king, a persecutor of Christians (Procopius c. 550; Munro-Hay 1991). From the seventh century, amid Persian and Arab conquests around the Red Sea, the coinage ended and power moved south, for reasons still debated. By the mid-twelfth century the Zagwe, an Agaw (Cushitic-speaking) dynasty from Lasta, held the throne; King Lalibela (around 1200) is credited with the rock-cut churches of Roha, now named after him. In 1270 Yekuno Amlak of Amhara overthrew the Zagwe. His Solomonic dynasty claimed descent from Solomon and the Queen of Sheba, as told in the fourteenth-century *Kebra Nagast* ("Glory of the Kings"). Amda Seyon (1314–1344) pushed south and in 1332 crushed a revolt of the Muslim sultanate of Ifat; Zara Yaqob (1434–1468) beat Ifat's successor, Adal, at Gomit (1445) and reformed the Church (Taddesse Tamrat 1972).

## War and society

Byzantine merchants such as Cosmas Indicopleustes traded at Adulis under Kaleb. The emperor Justinian asked the Aksumites, as fellow Christians, to buy Indian silk for his empire, bypassing Persia, but Persian merchants bought up whole cargoes where Indian ships first landed (Procopius c. 550). The Solomonic kings mostly ruled from a tented camp touring the provinces (Taddesse Tamrat 1972); there, in the 1520s, the Portuguese priest Álvares saw lords riding mules and porters carrying the treasure (Álvares 1540). From Amda Seyon onward the army's core was the *chewa*, the king's standing regiments of spearmen, archers and horsemen; the spear was the main weapon, and swords were rare (Taddesse Tamrat 1972). Instead of wages, kings granted soldiers, nobles and churches *gult*, the right to collect tribute, largely in kind, from a district's peasants (Crummey 2000). Ambas, flat-topped mountains with sheer sides, served as fortresses, treasuries and prisons: royal princes were confined on Amba Geshen to prevent succession wars. The Church, headed by a Coptic bishop (*abun*) sent from Egypt, kept Ge'ez for worship and writing. Monasteries such as Debre Damo, traditionally of the sixth century, and Debre Asbo (later Debre Libanos), founded by Takla Haymanot around 1284, ran schools and copied manuscripts; monks also evangelised the conquered south.

## End and legacy

In 1529, after two centuries of intermittent wars, Adal's leader Ahmad ibn Ibrahim, called Gragn ("the left-handed") by Ethiopians, defeated King Lebna Dengel at Shimbra Kure. His armies overran most of the highlands, burning churches and monasteries, Debre Libanos among them (1531), as his chronicler Arab Faqih relates. Some 400 Portuguese musketeers under Cristóvão da Gama landed in 1541. Ahmad, reinforced by Ottoman musketeers from Yemen, had Gama killed in 1542, but in February 1543 Lebna Dengel's son, King Galawdewos, and the surviving Portuguese defeated and killed Ahmad at Wayna Daga (Castanhoso 1564). The kingdom, which had owned only a handful of guns, slowly took up the musket. Kings claiming Solomonic descent reigned, at times only in name, until 1974.

## Contested

- **The shotel.** Secure evidence for the sickle-shaped sword of the game's Shotel Warrior, which the codex drops, dates mostly from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries; no known source shows that medieval Ethiopian swords were shotels, or that their curve was meant against horsemen and shields.
- **Spear or bow.** In the 1340s the Arab scholar al-Umari listed the bow but made the spear the main weapon (Taddesse Tamrat 1972); Amda Seyon's chronicle, the *Glorious Victories*, names archers beside spearmen and horsemen. How much the bow counted is uncertain.
- **Gudit and the Zagwe.** Gudit, a queen said to have burned Aksum's churches, is usually placed in the tenth century, when the Arab geographer Ibn Hawqal reported a woman ruling the Habasha. Some Ethiopian traditions make her a convert to Judaism; a reading summarised by Taddesse Tamrat (1972) makes her queen of pagan Damot, in the south. Conflicting traditions on how long the Zagwe ruled date their rise to about 940 or 1137 (Taddesse Tamrat 1972).

# Persians

## Who they were

The codex's Persians represent Iran from 500 to 1507: the late Sasanian Empire (the core, 500–651), then the Persian-speaking and Persianate states of Islamic Iran (Persianate: using Persian in court and government, even under Turkic or Mongol rulers). The Sasanians, from Pars (Fars) in the south-west, the origin of the name "Persia", overthrew the Parthians in 224 and called their realm Ērānshahr, "land of the Iranians". It joined the Iranian plateau to Mesopotamia and reached into Khorasan in the north-east. Its capital, Ctesiphon, stood on the Tigris, south-east of modern Baghdad. Later states also held Transoxiana, around Bukhara and Samarkand.

## Rise and height

Khosrow I (531–579) sacked Antioch (540). Around 560, allied with the Turks, he crushed the Hephthalites, Iran's eastern rivals, and around 570 won Yemen by sea (Daryaee 2009). Khosrow II (590–628) claimed to avenge his ally Maurice, the eastern Roman (Byzantine) emperor murdered in 602. His armies took Jerusalem (614) and Egypt (618–621) and camped opposite Constantinople during the Avar siege (626). The emperor Heraclius, campaigning in Persian territory since 624, won at Nineveh (627); Khosrow was overthrown in 628. Torn by civil war, the empire lost to the Arabs at Qadisiyya (636 or 637) and Nahavand (642); the last king, Yazdegerd III, was killed near Merv in 651. Yet Tabaristan, by the Caspian, held out into the eighth century.

From the ninth century Iranian dynasties rose again. The Samanids of Bukhara (819–999) patronised poetry in Persian, now written in Arabic script; the Buyids, Daylamite soldiers from the Caspian mountains, took Baghdad in 945 and ruled in the caliph's name. Turkic dynasties followed, the Ghaznavids and then the Seljuks, whose Persian vizier (chief minister, 1064–1092), Nizam al-Mulk, governed an empire from Transoxiana to Syria. Mongol conquerors, the Ilkhans, ruled Iran from 1256 to 1335. Timur (Tamerlane, 1370–1405) campaigned from Delhi to Ankara; his heirs made Samarkand and Herat centres of art and science, while the Turkmen Qara Qoyunlu and then Aq Qoyunlu confederations ruled western Iran from Tabriz.

## War and society

The asvaran ("horsemen"), armoured noble cavalry who fought with lance and bow, were the core of Sasanian armies. Procopius, a Roman historian, and the Strategikon (c. 600), a Roman military manual, both describe Persian archery as rapid but not powerful; Procopius adds that almost all Persians were archers. Later Turkic and Mongol armies relied above all on mounted archers. Indian elephants, which frightened the Arabs' horses, helped win the Battle of the Bridge (634). Walls shielded the north: the fifth- or sixth-century Wall of Gorgan ran some 195 km east from the Caspian, with over 30 forts (Sauer et al. 2013).

On the dry plateau, farms depended on qanats, gently sloping tunnels that bring groundwater to the surface. Canals watered the Sawad, Iraq's fertile plain. Al-Tabari reports that Khosrow I completed his father Kavad's survey of the farmland, taxing each field in silver by area and crop; the Arabs largely kept this land tax as the kharaj. Caravanserais (Persian kārvānsarā, "caravan house") sheltered merchants crossing Iran. Zoroastrianism was the Sasanian state religion, and abandoning it could be punished by death. Nizam al-Mulk's Nizamiyya colleges, such as Baghdad's (1067), spread the madrasa, a school of Islamic law (Makdisi 1981). From 1090 the Nizari Ismailis, a Shia movement, held mountain castles such as Alamut, which fell to the Mongols in 1256 (Daftary 1990).

## End and legacy

In 1501 Ismail, head of the Safavid order of Sufi mystics, took Tabriz and proclaimed himself shah; in 1507 Muhammad Shaybani's Uzbeks took Herat, ending Timurid rule in Khorasan. Persian long remained a language of poetry and government from Anatolia to India, and Ferdowsi's *Shahnameh* (1010), the "Book of Kings", is still Iran's national epic.

## Contested

- **Why the empire fell so fast.** Older studies saw a highly centralised Sasanian monarchy; Pourshariati (2008) argues that the kings ruled through a confederacy with great Parthian noble families, whose rivalries and defections opened Iran to the Arab conquest.
- **Christians in a Zoroastrian empire.** Syriac accounts of Christian martyrs and older scholarship present Christians as a marginal, often persecuted minority; Payne (2015) argues that Zoroastrian thought gave them a legitimate though subordinate place and that their elites were integrated into the empire's institutions.
- **The Wall of Gorgan.** No inscription names its builder. Its traditional names credit Alexander the Great (centuries too early) or the Sasanian kings Peroz (459–484) and Khosrow I, and some scholars thought it Parthian; the excavators' radiocarbon and luminescence dates place it between the 420s and 540s, most likely in the fifth century (Sauer et al. 2013).

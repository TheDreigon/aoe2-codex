# Rus'

## Who they were

The Rus' were the people and principalities ruled by the Rurikid dynasty in the forests and forest-steppe of eastern Europe, from Oleg's taking of Kiev (modern Kyiv), traditionally dated 882, to the death of Ivan III of Moscow in 1505. Their heartlands were Kiev, Novgorod, Galicia–Volhynia (roughly today's western Ukraine) and, in the upper Volga basin, Vladimir-Suzdal and Moscow. The name first belonged to a largely Scandinavian elite that ruled East Slavs and Finnic peoples and merged with them. The game calls them "Slavs".

## Rise and height

Treaties of 911 and 944 regulated Rus' dealings with Constantinople. Prince Sviatoslav (d. 972) broke the power of the Khazars, a Turkic people of the lower Volga, but lost a Danube war with Byzantium in 971. His son Vladimir (980–1015) was baptised, traditionally in 988, married the emperor's sister Anna and brought saints' relics from Chersonesos in Crimea to Kiev. Yaroslav the Wise (d. 1054) built St Sophia in Kiev. After 1132 no prince could hold the Rus' lands together. Novgorod, traditionally a republic from 1136, was run by its town assembly (veche) and boyars (nobles). In 1169 Andrey Bogolyubsky of Vladimir-Suzdal had Kiev sacked. After the Mongols took Vladimir (1238) and Kiev (1240), the princes ruled as tributaries of the Golden Horde, under the khans' charters (yarlyks). Daniel of Galicia was crowned king of Rus' in 1253. In the north-east Moscow rose: Ivan I (1325–1340), called Kalita ("money-bag"), became grand prince of Vladimir and was responsible for the Horde's tribute (Martin 2007). Ivan III (1462–1505) annexed Novgorod (1478) and Tver (1485). In 1480 the Great Horde, the Golden Horde's main successor, failed to cross the Ugra river against Muscovite arrows and guns and withdrew weeks later, an event traditionally seen as the end of its overlordship.

## War and society

Early armies centred on the prince's retinue, the druzhina. The Byzantine historian Leo the Deacon describes Sviatoslav's men fighting on foot in a shield wall in 971. From the eleventh century, if not earlier, the druzhina fought mainly on horseback, in mail and lamellar armour of laced plates (Kirpichnikov 1971). Its senior men, the boyars, became a landed aristocracy. Kiev settled Turkic nomads, later called the Black Hoods, on the Ros river as frontier horse archers from the late eleventh century. Muscovite cavalry fought like the Horde's horsemen, with bow and sabre (Ostrowski 1998). Towns grew around timber-and-earth citadels (detinets, later kremlin); Moscow's Kremlin gained stone walls in 1367–1368 and brick ones in 1485–1495. In 1382 Moscow's defenders fired guns at Khan Tokhtamysh, the first firearms recorded in Rus' hands, and by 1488 Moscow had a cannon house.

Most Rus' were farmers, clearing forest by slash-and-burn in the north and ploughing the fertile black earth of the south. Furs were their great export (Martin 1986): in the tenth century they traded furs and slaves for silver coins from the Islamic world. Around 950 the emperor Constantine VII described their boat convoy down the Dnieper to Constantinople. Later, Novgorod sold furs and wax to German Hanse merchants, whose trading post there Ivan III closed in 1494. Christianity came from Byzantium: a metropolitan (chief bishop), usually Greek, led the Church from Kiev, and its Slavic-language books came from Bulgaria (Franklin and Shepard 1996). From the fourteenth century Sergius of Radonezh and other monks founded monasteries deep in the northern forests.

## End and legacy

When Ivan III died in 1505 he styled himself sovereign of all Rus', yet Kiev and most western Rus' lands belonged to the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, and Galicia to Poland. Ukrainians, Belarusians and Russians all share the Rus' heritage, so the codex does not call them Russians.

## Contested

- **The Normanist debate.** "Normanists" credit Scandinavians with founding the Rus' state: a Frankish court identified envoys of the "Rhos" as Swedes in 839, and most Rus' names in the treaty of 911 are Norse. "Anti-Normanists", influential in Soviet scholarship, sought Slavic origins instead. Most scholars now see a Scandinavian elite that merged with the Slavs by the eleventh century (Franklin and Shepard 1996).
- **Kiev's heirs.** This text follows the line from Kiev through Vladimir-Suzdal to Moscow, the traditional scheme of Russian historians; the Ukrainian historian Mykhailo Hrushevsky (1904) traced Kiev's heritage instead through Galicia–Volhynia and the Grand Duchy of Lithuania to the Ukrainians.
- **The Horde's legacy.** Ostrowski (1998) argues that Muscovy took much of its civil and military organisation from the Mongols; much Russian and Soviet scholarship stressed instead the destruction of the conquest and the native roots of Muscovite institutions.

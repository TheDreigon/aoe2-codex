# Arabs

## Who they were

The codex's Arabs run from 632, when Muhammad's first successor (caliph) took power, to the Ottoman conquest of Egypt (1517). They cover the Rashidun, Umayyad, Abbasid and Fatimid caliphates, then the Ayyubid and Mamluk sultanates of Egypt and Syria, the civ's military core (1171–1500). The codex places them in Arabia, Syria, Iraq, Egypt and the southern and eastern Mediterranean, with al-Andalus (Islamic Iberia) only as an Umayyad province (711–750). The Ayyubids were Kurds. The Mamluk elite were mostly Turkic Kipchaks and, from 1382, Circassians from the Caucasus; contemporaries called their state "the state of the Turks", though it ruled Arabic speakers in Arabic. The game's "Saracens" was an outsiders' word: Roman-era writers used it for Arabian nomads, medieval Christians for all Muslims.

## Rise and height

The caliphs' tribal armies, led by the elite of Mecca and Medina, attacked Byzantium and Persia. They won at Yarmuk (636), took Syria, Iraq and Egypt within a decade and ended the Persian Empire by 651 (Kennedy 2007). After a civil war (656–661) against the caliph Ali, Mu'awiya, governor of Syria, founded the Umayyad dynasty at Damascus. The caliph Abd al-Malik (685–705) built the Dome of the Rock, struck gold dinars with words instead of images (696/697) and began putting the government registers (*diwan*) into Arabic. The Abbasids, who overthrew the Umayyads in 750, founded Baghdad in 762. From 945 Iranian Buyids, then Turkish Seljuks, held Baghdad in the Abbasid caliph's name. The Fatimids, rival Shia caliphs, took Egypt and founded Cairo (969). Saladin, founder of the Ayyubid dynasty, ended the Fatimid caliphate (1171), joined Syria to Egypt and recovered Jerusalem from the crusaders (1187). In 1250 the slave soldiers (mamluks) of the late sultan al-Salih Ayyub killed his son and took power. They beat the Mongols at Ain Jalut in 1260 and Homs in 1281 (Amitai-Preiss 1995) and took Acre, the last crusader capital, in 1291.

## War and society

The conquerors fought mostly on horseback or on foot; camels carried men and water, as on Khalid ibn al-Walid's desert march to Syria (634), in al-Baladhuri's ninth-century account. Arab fleets beat the Byzantines at the Battle of the Masts (654/655), and state arsenals (*dar al-sina'a*, "house of manufacture") kept Muslim galleys strong into the eleventh century. Merchants linked the Mediterranean, Indian Ocean and Sahara. Most subjects long stayed Christian, Jewish or Zoroastrian, as protected tributaries (*dhimmis*). From al-Mansur's reign (754–775), Abbasid caliphs and courtiers had Greek, Persian and Indian works translated into Arabic (Gutas 1998). Hospitals (*bimaristan*, Persian for "place of the sick") appeared in Baghdad around 800 (Dols 1987).

Like the Abbasids from the 830s, the Mamluk sultans relied on slave soldiers: boys bought around the Black Sea, trained in horsemanship, archery, lance and sword (*furusiyya*), then freed. They fought above all as fast-shooting horse archers. Amirs (commanders) maintained their mamluks from an *iqta'*, a district's tax revenue assigned in place of pay: an amir "of forty" was to keep forty. Al-Tarsusi's treatise for Saladin (c. 1187) gives one of the earliest clear descriptions of the counterweight trebuchet, which the Mamluks massed against crusader walls. Cannon served in sieges from the 1360s, but in 1498 the royal mamluks broke up a corps of enslaved Africans armed with handguns (Ayalon 1956).

## End and legacy

After the Mongols sacked Baghdad (1258), Abbasid caliphs lived on in Cairo as the sultans' figureheads. Plague from 1347, Timur's sack of Damascus (1400–1401) and Portuguese rivalry in the Indian Ocean strained the sultanate. The Ottomans under Selim I conquered it, defeating the sultans al-Ghawri at Marj Dabiq (1516) and Tumanbay outside Cairo (1517). Arabic is still spoken from Iraq to Morocco, and English "arsenal", "algebra" and "algorithm" come from it, the last two through twelfth-century Latin translations of al-Khwarizmi.

## Contested

- **Firearms.** Ayalon (1956) argued that the mamluk horsemen's contempt for handguns helped the Ottomans defeat them in 1516–1517. Irwin (2004), drawing on Western sources that Ayalon had not used, argues that the Mamluks did adopt firearms when these offered some benefit, but that, for material and logistical reasons, guns often failed to give the clear advantage expected of them.
- **The House of Wisdom.** Popular accounts make al-Ma'mun's House of Wisdom (*Bayt al-Hikma*) a scientific academy at the heart of the translations; Gutas (1998) argues that it was mainly a library, and that patrons across Baghdad's elite paid for the translations.
- **The first hospital.** Some accounts credit the Umayyad caliph al-Walid I with a hospital at Damascus in 707; Dols (1987) reads the sources as describing something closer to a leper house, in line with Byzantine practice, and places the first true hospital in Baghdad under Harun al-Rashid (786–809).

# Swedes

## Who they were

The codex's Swedes are the Svear of Uppland and the Mälaren region, the Götar (Geats) of Götaland, and the kingdom that grew from them, which took in Finland from the thirteenth century. *Sverige* means "realm of the Svear". Their window, 500 to 1500, runs from Vendel-period Uppland through the Viking-age ventures across the Baltic to the struggle with the Kalmar Union. The game's name, "Varangians", from Old Norse *væringi* ("sworn man"), is what Greeks, Rus' and Arabs called Scandinavians abroad; first recorded in the eleventh century, it was never a people's own name.

## Rise and height

The historian Jordanes (551) says the Suehans, usually taken to be the Svear, had excellent horses and sent dark furs to the Romans through many other peoples. In the Vendel period (c. 550–800) Uppland's warrior elite was buried in boats at Vendel and Valsgärde with helmets, weapons and horses. Around 750 over forty men, envoys or raiders, died violently and were buried in two ships at Salme in Estonia; isotopes in their teeth suggest they came from the Mälaren region (Price et al. 2016). Birka on Lake Mälaren (c. 750–975) traded along the rivers of eastern Europe. In 839 *Rhos* (Rus') who reached the Frankish court with Byzantine envoys were found to be "of the people of the Swedes" (*Annales Bertiniani*). Furs and slaves bought Islamic silver coins (Franklin and Shepard 1996); a hoard buried at Spillings on Gotland after 870 holds over 14,000 of them. Olof Skötkonung (c. 995–1022) was the first Swedish king to stay Christian. Some 26 runestones remember men lost with Ingvar's fleet in the east around 1041. Yet the kingdom was consolidated only in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries (Line 2007). Birger Jarl, the king's first officer (*jarl*) from 1248 to 1266, is credited with founding Stockholm, first named in 1252.

## War and society

Free peasants met in local assemblies (*things*) and owed military service. Under the provincial laws of Uppland (1296) and Södermanland (1327), districts fitted out oared warships for the levy fleet (*ledung*). Each *hamna*, a group of farmers who supplied one crewman, armed him with shield, sword, spear and iron hat, as the Södermanland law specifies. In the Alsnö statute (1279 or 1280), long seen as the birth of the nobility (*frälse*), King Magnus Ladulås freed from royal taxes all men who served with a war horse. He also forbade great men to lodge on peasants without paying. Bergslagen, a mining district, had early blast furnaces such as Lapphyttan (c. 1150–1350). Its iron and Falun's copper (first recorded in 1288) went through Stockholm to Lübeck and the German trading towns of the Hanse. King Erik (d. 1160) became the realm's patron saint, and St Birgitta (d. 1373) founded the Bridgettine order, based at Vadstena. In 1483 Johann Snell printed Sweden's first book, at Stockholm.

## End and legacy

In 1397 the Kalmar Union joined Denmark, Norway and Sweden under one king, Erik of Pomerania, crowned at Kalmar. His wars raised taxes and hurt the iron trade, and in 1434 the mine owner Engelbrekt Engelbrektsson led the miners and peasants of Bergslagen and Dalarna against the bailiffs who held the king's castles. The rising spread, drew in magnates and took many castles; in January 1435 a meeting at Arboga named him captain of the realm (*rikshövitsman*), and in 1436 he was murdered. In 1471 the regent Sten Sture the Elder, with peasant levies, Stockholm's burghers and noble horsemen, routed Christian I of Denmark at Brunkeberg; Swedish tradition holds that a shot broke several of the king's teeth. Sweden left the union for good in 1523, under Gustav Vasa. The peasants kept a political voice, and Engelbrekt became a national hero.

## Contested

- **The Rus'.** The *Rhos* of 839 and the usual derivation of *Rus'* from Finnish *Ruotsi* ("Sweden") lead most historians to see the first Rus' as Scandinavians (Franklin and Shepard 1996). Anti-Normanism, official in Soviet scholarship and revived in some recent Russian work, rejects this "Normanist" view and stresses the state's Slavic roots.
- **The Alsnö statute.** Older scholarship saw it as the birth of the secular nobility; more recent research sees it as extending the tax freedom the king's own men already had to all who served on horseback, whomever they served. Its date, May 1279 or September 1280, is also disputed.
- **Engelbrekt's rising.** Nineteenth-century national and liberal historians idealised the free, armed peasant and made Engelbrekt the founder of a national democracy. Lönnroth (1934) saw a struggle between crown and aristocracy, with national slogans as mere propaganda, and accepted a 1450s date for the anti-Danish rhymed Engelbrekt chronicle; Bagge (2016), noting that it is now dated to just after the events, argues that national feeling was a real part of contemporary politics.

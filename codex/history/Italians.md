# Italians

## Who they were

The codex's Italians are the city-states of northern and central Italy from about 1000 to 1494: the maritime republics of Venice, Genoa and Pisa, inland cities such as Milan and Florence, and Venetian and Genoese colonies from Crete to the Crimea. Northern and central Italy and Flanders were Catholic Europe's most urban regions; the chronicler Giovanni Villani put Florence's population at some 90,000 around 1338. The cities never formed one state. Most lay in the Kingdom of Italy, tied to the German crown since 962, while Venice had grown out of Byzantine rule. People saw themselves first as Venetians, Genoese or Florentines; northern Europeans often called all Italian merchants "Lombards".

## Rise and height

In 1000 Venice's doge (elected duke), Pietro II Orseolo, won the Dalmatian towns' loyalty with a fleet. Sworn associations of citizens elected leaders called consuls: at Pisa from the 1080s, then at Genoa, Milan and many other cities by the 1130s. These associations grew into self-governing communes. Genoese fleets backed the First Crusade (1096–1099), and Genoa and Pisa won trading quarters in the crusader states. The Lombard League of cities beat Emperor Frederick Barbarossa at Legnano (1176), and at Constance (1183) he recognised their self-government. In 1204 the Fourth Crusade, carried by Doge Enrico Dandolo's fleet, sacked Constantinople, and Venice gained Crete and other bases (Lane 1973). Allied with Byzantium in 1261, Genoa settled Caffa in the Crimea and Pera, facing Constantinople. It crushed Pisa at Meloria (1284) and fought Venice repeatedly until the war of Chioggia in 1378–1381 (Epstein 1996). Inland, many communes came under lords (*signori*): the Visconti became dukes of Milan in 1395, while the Medici dominated republican Florence from 1434. The Peace of Lodi (1454) kept Italy in uneasy balance for forty years.

## War and society

Citizens guarded their walls and served in militias, rallying around the *carroccio*, an ox-drawn wagon with the city's banner and an altar. At Legnano the Milanese infantry held firm around theirs. The cities' main missile troops were crossbowmen, who from the thirteenth century shot from behind the pavise, a tall shield probably named after Pavia. Genoese crossbowmen served on galleys and abroad: several thousand opened the French attack at Crécy (1346). From the fourteenth century, cities hired mercenary companies by contract (*condotta*) under captains (*condottieri*) such as the Englishman John Hawkwood. Their core was heavy cavalry in "lances" of a mounted man-at-arms, a squire and a page. In the fifteenth century the men-at-arms wore Milanese plate armour, and Venice and Milan kept them on long contracts, as almost permanent armies (Mallett 1974). A Florentine decree of 1326 is among Europe's earliest records of cannon, and at Chioggia guns were fired from ships.

Venice's state shipyard, the Arsenal, built war galleys and the merchant galleys of yearly convoys to Alexandria and Flanders. Voyages were often financed by the *commenda*: a stay-at-home investor funded a travelling merchant for a share of the profit (Lopez 1971). Merchants developed the bill of exchange, for paying sums abroad, and double-entry bookkeeping. Florence's florin (1252) and Venice's ducat (1284) became international gold coins, and banks such as the Bardi and Medici lent to popes and kings. Universities rose at Bologna (by tradition 1088) and Padua (1222).

## End and legacy

The Ottomans took Genoese Pera (1453) and Caffa (1475) and Venetian Negroponte (Euboea, 1470). In 1494 Charles VIII of France invaded Italy to claim Naples, opening the Italian Wars (1494–1559). That year the Medici were expelled from Florence, and their bank, founded in 1397, came to an end (de Roover 1963). Venice remained an independent republic until 1797. The cities gave Europe banking and accounting techniques, a tradition of civic self-government and the art and learning of the Renaissance.

## Contested

- **The condottieri.** Machiavelli (1513) called mercenaries useless and dangerous, disloyal and cowardly, and blamed them for Italy's ruin; Mallett (1974) argues that historians long underrated the efficiency of fifteenth-century Italian armies, whose captains had become more stable and professional.
- **How the communes began.** Older histories date each commune from the first document naming consuls; Wickham (2015) argues that the communes took shape gradually over the following decades, without their leaders realising they were creating something new.
- **Bologna's 1088.** No founding document exists: the date was fixed for the eighth-centenary celebrations of 1888 by a committee led by the poet Giosuè Carducci, who himself admitted that the true date could not be known.
